The Candidate That Walked Away

10 May 2026 4 min read

On 28 March 2026, Georgian Dream initiated constitutional court proceedings to ban three opposition parties — the United National Movement, Coalition for Change, and Strong Georgia — representing roughly half the opposition electorate. Prime Minister Irakli Kobakhidze publicly vowed to ban more than half a dozen parties in total. The move came less than two years after Georgia received EU candidate status and eight months after the same government froze its own accession talks until 2028. A country that Brussels welcomed as a future member is now dismantling the democratic criteria that made candidacy possible — and the EU's enlargement toolkit has no protocol for a candidate that walks away.

The structural problem is not that Georgia is drifting toward Moscow. It is that the institutional mechanisms Brussels designed to pull candidates toward Copenhagen criteria assume the candidate wants to converge. When the candidate government chooses divergence instead, the accession process offers no enforcement lever beyond suspension — a lever Georgia has already pulled on itself.

The legislative arc from fines to prison

Georgia's foreign agents law, passed in May 2024, initially carried administrative fines for organisations receiving foreign funding deemed to serve a foreign power. By March 2026, the law had been escalated to carry criminal penalties of up to five years' imprisonment. Seven NGO bank accounts were frozen in August 2025. The escalation from administrative to criminal sanctions is a deliberate legislative trajectory — each amendment widening the scope and sharpening the penalty.

The European Commission's response was calibrated. EU ambassador Paweł Herczyński announced in July 2024 that Georgia's accession process was halted. Tens of millions in financial assistance were frozen. The EU also formed a four-party South Caucasus contact group that pointedly excluded Georgia — a diplomatic signal that Brussels was rerouting regional engagement around Tbilisi rather than through it.

The foreign agents law was the trigger, but the broader pattern is state capture. Bidzina Ivanishvili, Georgian Dream's founder, holds no official title but controls the party's parliamentary majority. President Mikheil Kavelashvili, a former footballer with no prior political experience, was installed by a GD-dominated electoral college without a popular vote. The constitutional court is packed with loyalists. Over one hundred political prisoners have been imprisoned. The institutions that would normally check executive power have been absorbed by it.

The transatlantic sanctions gap

The United States sanctioned Ivanishvili personally on 27 December 2024 for undermining Georgian democracy. The European Parliament voted for equivalent sanctions, but the Council has not implemented them. Hungary blocks the measure — not out of solidarity with Tbilisi, but to avoid setting a precedent for state-capture sanctions that could later be applied within the EU itself. The result is a transatlantic policy gap: Washington has a named individual sanction in place; Brussels has a parliamentary resolution and a diplomatic freeze.

The Kulevi port episode illustrates the complexity. The EU initially proposed including Georgia's Kulevi oil terminal in its 20th sanctions package against Russia in February 2026. The terminal was removed after Georgia and SOCAR made compliance commitments — but the episode revealed Georgia's role as a conduit for Russian oil shipments circumventing existing sanctions. A candidate country is simultaneously subject to EU conditionality and facilitating sanctions evasion for the state Brussels identifies as its greatest strategic threat. The conditionality framework was not designed for this overlap.

Marginalisation by the neighbourhood

Georgia's marginalisation is accelerating from the outside as well. Armenia and Azerbaijan are advancing a US-backed peace process and the TRIPP corridor, which would erode Georgia's transit monopoly between the Black Sea and the Caspian. Georgia was absent from Davos 2026 while its neighbours presented themselves as regional architects. The Caucasus Watch analysis published in May 2026 frames the central risk as sidelining rather than isolation — Georgia is not being shut out, it is becoming irrelevant to the regional arrangements that will define the South Caucasus.

Seventy-four per cent of Georgians favour EU membership, according to polling, but have no institutional mechanism to translate that preference into policy. The protests of 2024 drew hundreds of thousands and changed nothing. The democratic deficit is not between Georgia and Brussels; it is between the Georgian public and the Georgian state.

The enlargement framework was built to reward convergence. It has no protocol for managing divergence by a candidate that controls its own suspension. Whether the EU develops one — or continues to treat Georgia's case as an exceptional pause rather than a structural gap in the accession architecture — will shape how every aspiring member state reads the credibility of Article 49.
