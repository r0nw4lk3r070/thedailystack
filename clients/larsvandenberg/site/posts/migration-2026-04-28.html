The Pact Meets the Surge: EU Migration Rules Face the Iran Crisis

28 April 2026 4 min read

On 12 June 2026, the EU's Migration and Asylum Pact becomes operational law across all member states. It is the most comprehensive overhaul of European asylum rules since the Common European Asylum System was built in the shadow of the 2015 crisis. Six weeks before that deadline, Alberto Horst Neidhardt of the European Policy Centre delivered a verdict Brussels was not prepared to hear: the new system will not cope with mass displacement. Nations could again resort to border closures.

The warning is not theoretical. Since US-Israeli strikes on Iran began in early March, IOM has reported nearly one million displaced people in Lebanon alone. Before the war, the EU Asylum Agency warned that even partial destabilisation of Iran — a country of 90 million — could generate refugee movements of an unprecedented magnitude. The Pact was negotiated for steady-state migration flows. It is about to meet a surge.

What the Pact changes

The Pact replaces much of the existing Common European Asylum System with four interlocking mechanisms. Standardised border screening applies to every irregular arrival — identity, security, and health checks within a fixed timeframe. Border asylum procedures must conclude within 12 weeks, including appeal. A solidarity pool, valued at €420 million annually, lets member states contribute either by accepting relocations or by paying €20,000 per person they refuse to take. Return hubs in non-EU countries are designed to accelerate deportations of failed asylum seekers.

The architecture is coherent on paper. Each mechanism carries a structural weakness the Iran crisis is already exposing.

The solidarity problem

The cash-out option is the Pact's most contested feature. A member state can pay €20,000 per person to avoid relocation obligations. During a surge, wealthy member states can buy their way out while frontline states — Italy, Greece, Cyprus — absorb the arrivals. The European Policy Centre warned in December 2024 that divergent national practices could undermine the entire solidarity framework. The Iran crisis turns that warning into a live scenario.

Cyprus, which currently holds the EU presidency, expresses cautious optimism. The Netherlands does too. Hungary and Italy push deterrence-first models. Germany previously reinstated border controls unilaterally. The Pact was adopted without unanimous Council support. If the solidarity pool fails under pressure, there is no fallback mechanism — only the unilateral measures the Pact was designed to replace.

Return hubs — the weakest link

Return hubs operate through bilateral agreements with non-EU countries. There is no overarching EU-wide binding framework governing how they function. Current enforcement rates tell the story: only 20–30% of roughly 700,000 annual return orders are carried out. In December 2025, the Council overturned protections for families with children facing return. UNHCR's Elizabeth Tan flagged "serious protection concerns" regarding hubs in Tunisia and Libya. Over 250 civil-society organisations have warned of arbitrary detention, chain refoulement, and weakened judicial review.

The Pact's screening and border procedures also narrow procedural rights. Biometric surveillance through Eurodac is expanded to children from age six. The deterrence logic is explicit. Whether it works is another matter — and whether it complies with the EU's own fundamental rights charter is a question the Court of Justice may eventually have to answer.

Implementation gaps

Member states submitted National Implementation Plans by December 2024. Most remain undisclosed. An international conference at Mykolas Romeris University in Vilnius on 23–24 April 2026 — four days before this article — highlighted that many legal, operational, and financial questions remain unresolved weeks before the Pact takes effect. The Commission has pushed compliance hard. Von der Leyen made it a priority. But compliance without capacity is a procedural achievement, not an operational one.

The Pact's crisis mechanism exists on paper. Whether it functions when hundreds of thousands move at once is the question the Iran crisis has already forced onto the agenda — before the Pact is even operational.
