The Netherlands Builds Europe's First National Code Forge

27 April 2026 4 min read

On 24 April 2026, the Dutch government soft-launched code.overheid.nl — a self-hosted, Forgejo-based code platform for government open-source software. No EU member state has done this before. The platform is designed to remove public code from infrastructure the Netherlands cannot control.

The Open Source Program Office (OSPO) at the Ministry of the Interior and Kingdom Relations (BZK) initiated the project. The premise is straightforward: hosting government source code on platforms you do not control is a risk the Netherlands is no longer willing to accept.

Why GitHub and GitLab fell short

The OSPO published a detailed recommendation in November 2025 explaining why neither GitHub nor GitLab met the government's requirements.

GitHub was ruled out first. It is proprietary software — not free, not open source. That conflicts with the Dutch government's own policy that open source should be preferred where equally suitable. The deeper concern is sovereignty. "The government cannot afford the risk of code or binaries in repositories being tampered with," the OSPO wrote. When you host code on a platform you do not control, you cannot guarantee the integrity of what developers download and run.

The ICC prosecutor incident in May 2025 made this concrete. Microsoft blocked the email account of the ICC's chief prosecutor, demonstrating that platform providers can weaponise infrastructure for diplomatic purposes. For a government managing critical digital infrastructure, that is an unacceptable dependency.

GitLab seemed like a better fit but was rejected for a different reason: vendor lock-in. GitLab operates on an open-core model. The Community Edition is free software, but the Enterprise Edition — which adds security scanning, compliance features, and scalability — is proprietary. Organisations that start on the free tier gradually migrate to paid, locked-in features. The OSPO concluded that this lock-in path contradicts digital sovereignty.

Why Forgejo

Forgejo, a fork of Gitea created in 2022 after Gitea incorporated as a for-profit company, met all the requirements. It is fully free software, governed by Codeberg e.V., a non-profit based in Germany. Every line of its source code can be studied, modified, and redistributed. It uses the European Public Licence. The OSPO framed the choice plainly: "Public money, public code."

Forgejo is also lighter than GitLab, which makes it easier to self-host and maintain — a practical advantage for a government that wants full operational control. But that lightness comes with trade-offs: fewer built-in features for CI/CD pipelines, project management, and enterprise-scale support.

Pilot phase, not finished product

code.overheid.nl is live but not yet open to all government organisations. The OSPO chose a gradual approach: start with a limited group of early adopters, build features together, and expand from there. Developers are invited to contribute via codeplatform@rijksoverheid.nl.

Boris van Hoytema, who works at the OSPO, emphasised that the platform is being built with its users, not for them. The risk of building without the target audience is that you create something nobody uses. By involving organisations from the start, the OSPO hopes to build features that civil servants actually need.

The platform currently lacks the maturity of GitHub or GitLab. There are no mature CI/CD pipelines, no advanced project management tools, and no enterprise support contracts. Some Dutch municipalities already run self-hosted GitLab instances — more feature-rich and already more sovereign than GitHub. Forgejo's advantage is its licensing model, not its feature set.

The sovereignty context

This is not an isolated IT project. The launch of code.overheid.nl fits into a broader Dutch and European push for digital sovereignty. The Netherlands' Digitalisation Strategy 2025 explicitly calls for reducing dependence on foreign cloud and SaaS platforms. Across the EU, the Gaia-X initiative, the Cyber Resilience Act, and the proposed European Cloud Federation all point in the same direction: public infrastructure should not depend on providers subject to another jurisdiction's laws.

The Netherlands is the first EU member state to act on this logic for code hosting. If the pilot succeeds, it creates a model that other member states could replicate — a national Forgejo instance, interoperable with others, governed by public-interest rules rather than corporate terms of service.

What comes next

The real test is whether code.overheid.nl can scale from pilot to government-wide platform. That requires not just technical maturity but organisational buy-in: government bodies need to migrate their repositories, adapt their workflows, and accept that a lighter platform means fewer conveniences.

The OSPO is betting that sovereignty outweighs convenience. The gap between a principle and a working platform is where most government IT projects fail. The Netherlands has institutional memory here — developer.overheid.nl has hosted open-source repositories for years, and the OSPO itself was established to professionalise how the government publishes and maintains public code.

Whether Forgejo, in its current state, is robust enough to become the backbone of a country's public software infrastructure — that is the question the Dutch government is now answering in practice.
