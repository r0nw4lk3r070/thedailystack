Not in Our Cloud: Germany's Bundeswehr Draws the Line on Palantir

29 April 2026 3 min read

Vice Admiral Thomas Daum did not mince words. Asked whether Palantir would power the Bundeswehr's new military cloud, Germany's cyber commander gave a flat no: "Das sehe ich momentan überhaupt nicht." The rejection is not a procurement preference. It is a sovereignty statement — and it exposes a fault line running through every NATO capital that depends on American AI infrastructure.

The project

The cloud at stake is central: a protected private cloud for data processing and artificial intelligence, described by the Bundeswehr as indispensable for modern digital defence. This is the digital backbone of a military rearming after decades of underinvestment. Who builds it is not a technicality.

Palantir's Maven platform was the obvious candidate. It aggregates battlefield data from multiple sources into a single picture commanders can act on. It is operational at NATO. Several allies use it. The Pentagon signed a framework agreement with Palantir in July 2025 — up to $10 billion over ten years, consolidating 75 contracts, effectively making Maven the operating system for key US military functions. Adopting it would give the Bundeswehr plug-and-play interoperability with its most important allies.

The catch

That interoperability comes with a condition. At NATO, Palantir employees operate Maven directly inside military systems. They have access to the data. For Daum, that is unacceptable on a national cloud. Granting a private company — subject to US law — access to classified German military data is, in his words, "unvorstellbar."

The legal backbone of that concern is the US CLOUD Act. Passed in 2018, it allows American authorities to compel US-based companies to hand over data regardless of where it is stored. A Palantir-hosted military cloud on German soil would still be reachable by Washington through a court order served on Palantir's headquarters in Denver. No European server, no German data protection law, no constitutional safeguard changes that calculus. For a military building its digital infrastructure from scratch, that is a red line.

The double standard

What makes the decision sharp is the gap it reveals. German police in Bavaria, Hesse, and North Rhine-Westphalia already use Palantir software for investigative analytics. Civilian security agencies have accepted the CLOUD Act risk that the Bundeswehr refuses to tolerate. The military's higher threshold is logical — military data carries stakes that police data does not — but the gap raises uncomfortable questions about what civilian agencies have quietly conceded.

The alternatives

Three European companies are now in the running. Almato, based in Stuttgart and a subsidiary of Datagroup, is the establishment candidate. Orcrist, a Berlin startup, represents the domestic innovation pitch. ChapsVision, headquartered in Paris, brings a Franco-German dimension. All three will be tested this summer. A contract award is expected before the end of the year.

None of them has Palantir's track record. Maven is battle-tested, integrated across NATO, backed by a decade of US defence contracts. The European alternatives are smaller, less proven, and will need to demonstrate they can handle the scale and security requirements of a national military cloud. The Bundeswehr is betting sovereignty is worth the capability gap — and the integration cost.

The manifesto

Timing matters. In April 2026, Palantir published a 22-point manifesto summarising CEO Alex Karp's The Technological Republic. It declared that Silicon Valley has "an affirmative obligation" to defend the United States, called some cultures "dysfunctional and regressive," and argued that AI weapons are the new deterrence. The backlash was immediate. UK MPs called it the "ramblings of a supervillain." Whether the manifesto directly triggered Daum's decision or not, it reinforced the perception that Palantir is not a neutral vendor but an instrument of American power — and that adopting its platform means accepting the political assumptions baked into it.

The interoperability question

The unresolved question is NATO interoperability. If Germany builds a European AI cloud while its allies stay on Maven, the technical friction is real. Data-sharing, joint targeting workflows, common operational pictures — all depend on systems that can talk to each other. A European platform that cannot seamlessly exchange data with Maven is a strategic liability in a coalition. Germany will need either to build robust interfaces or to convince allies to follow its lead. Neither is easy.

The Bundeswehr has made its choice. The cloud will be European. The question now is whether that choice becomes a precedent — and whether Europe's defence industry can deliver on the sovereignty promise before the capability gap becomes a security gap.
