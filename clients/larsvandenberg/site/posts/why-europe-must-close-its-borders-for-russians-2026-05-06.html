Two Schengens, One Incoherent Policy: Russian Visas After the Ban

06 May 2026 4 min read

On 15 September 2025, Commissioner Magnus Brunner told the European Parliament that the European Union "does not have the ability to completely ban their issuance" — referring to Russian visas. Two months later, the Commission banned multi-entry Schengen visas for Russian nationals. The measure barely dented the numbers. Over 540,000 Schengen visas were issued to Russians in 2024, a 25% increase on the previous year. Italy, France, and Spain accounted for the bulk. The Baltics, meanwhile, had effectively sealed their borders. The policy is not merely inconsistent. It is structurally incoherent — and that incoherence carries consequences for both security and the dissidents the EU claims to support.

Article 14 of the Schengen Borders Code and Regulation (EC) No 810/2009 reserve visa decisions for individual Member States. The Commission can issue guidelines and tighten criteria, but it cannot override a sovereign decision by Rome or Paris to welcome Russian tourists. This is not a bureaucratic footnote. It is the legal architecture that allows GRU-directed sabotage to operate alongside mass tourism in the same zone. AP has tracked 145 Russian sabotage and disruption incidents across Europe since 2022 — 26 in 2024 alone, up from 1 in 2023. The campaign relies on proxy recruits, not visa-holding tourists. A tourist ban would not stop the bombs. It would trap the very dissidents the EU claims to protect.

The ban that changed nothing

The November 2025 multi-entry visa ban left single-entry tourist visas untouched — which means it left the vast majority of Russian visa traffic untouched. Russians received over 540,000 Schengen visas in 2024. The multi-entry restriction, Commissioner Brunner acknowledged on 15 September 2025, was the limit of what the Commission could do unilaterally. He said it plainly: the EU "does not have the ability to completely ban their issuance." The legal authority rests with Member States, not Brussels. The ban was a signal, not a solution. And the signal was received differently in different capitals.

The Cold War precedent

Targeted screening has historically outperformed blanket prohibition — and the US Cold War record proves it. In 1952, the United States imposed travel restrictions on Soviet citizens, creating "closed zones" modelled on Soviet practice and enforced on a reciprocity basis. A decade later, Secretary of State Dean Rusk recommended to President Kennedy that these restrictions be abolished. Rusk's reasoning was precise: the restrictions had become "increasingly ineffectual," failed to extract Soviet concessions, and forced so many exceptions that the policy was self-defeating. The 1962 reform kept real security controls — advance notification for long-term visitors, coordinated itineraries, closed zones for officials — but dropped the blanket prohibition on tourists and exchange visitors. Targeted screening preserves both security oversight and the channels that allow dissidents and defectors to reach the West. Blanket prohibition does neither. The lesson is unambiguous.

Two Schengens

The Schengen area now functions as two different visa regimes depending on which border you approach. Finland, Estonia, Latvia, and Lithuania have effectively closed their borders to Russian tourists. Finland has paid an economic price — including business bankruptcies in border regions. Poland received only 254 Russian visa applications in 2024, approving 98.82%. That is less a sign of openness than of deterrence: front-line states do not refuse applications because applications do not arrive. Italy, France, and Spain, meanwhile, issued the vast majority of the 540,000 visas granted in 2024, overwhelmingly for tourism. Greece and Hungary opposed von der Leyen's proposal for further restrictions, citing tourism revenue and Russian community ties. The result is a Schengen area that functions as a sealed border in Narva and an open door in Naples. The EU lacks the institutional mechanism to reconcile the two.

The structural question the Commission has yet to answer is whether the Schengen system can build a visa regime that distinguishes between a GRU proxy recruited on Telegram and a Russian opposition activist whose organisation the Kremlin is seeking to designate as terrorist. Rusk's 1962 reform worked because it replaced a blanket policy with a targeted one — and because the United States had the institutional capacity to enforce the distinction. The Schengen system, as currently structured, has no such capacity. Each Member State screens its own applicants, against its own criteria, with its own intelligence inputs. Until that coordination gap is addressed, the EU will continue to pursue a policy that is simultaneously too open to be secure and too closed to be just.
