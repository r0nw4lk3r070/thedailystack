The Conditionality Test

29 April 2026 4 min read

On 29 April 2026, the European Parliament voted 418 to 207 to call on the European Commission to trigger the rule-of-law conditionality mechanism against Slovakia. If the Commission acts — and the Council approves by qualified majority — billions in EU funding could be frozen. The resolution, embedded in the routine 2024 budget discharge procedure, was led by German Greens MEP Daniel Freund. It marks the first time the Parliament has formally demanded conditionality proceedings against Slovakia.

What Slovakia has dismantled

Since returning to power in October 2023 for a fourth term, Prime Minister Robert Fico has moved methodically against the institutions that held his government to account. The Special Prosecutor's Office — which handled high-level corruption and organised crime cases — was abolished in 2024 as part of a broader criminal justice reform. The same reform rewrote the penal code, enabling the release of hundreds of convicted corruption offenders. The National Criminal Agency (NAKA), an elite police unit tasked with fighting organised crime, was dismantled alongside it. The 2024 EU Rule of Law Report noted that fewer corruption cases would reach the replacement unit, with more diverted to lower-level district police directorates.

In December 2025, on International Anti-Corruption Day, the Slovak parliament fast-tracked a bill abolishing the Whistleblower Protection Office. The law cut short the tenure of director Zuzana Dlugošová, who had repeatedly warned Slovak officials that the plans breached EU law. The replacement institution's leadership would be politically appointed. Slovakia's Constitutional Court paused the law for review. On 30 January 2026, the European Commission launched a formal infringement procedure, stating it "considers that this law breaches EU rules." A separate infringement procedure, launched in November 2025, targets Slovakia's constitutional amendment enshrining only two genders.

Fico has not concealed his intentions. He has openly said he wants to follow Viktor Orbán's model. His government has escalated, not reversed, the crackdown.

The Hacienda scandal and the subsidy loophole

The resolution is not only about institutional dismantling. It also responds to systematic misuse of EU tourism subsidies — the "Hacienda" scandal. EU funds meant to promote rural tourism were used to build and renovate luxury villas owned by people close to Fico. The properties were never genuinely accessible to the public. Under current EU rules, subsidised properties can pass into private ownership after just five years — a loophole Freund argues "practically invites abuse and corruption."

Zuzana Šubová, who worked at the authority distributing agricultural subsidies, co-exposed the scandal. After she spoke with Freund and a television crew, 13 police officers arrived at her door, seized her devices and documents, and held her for a day. She was not deterred: on 27 January 2026, she appeared before the Parliament's Budgetary Control Committee, accompanied by representatives of Stop Corruption. The resolution also notes a prior OLAF finding that led to a €1.225 million reduction in EU support, and flags suspected fraud involving EU-funded pensions linked to government allies.

Why Slovak opposition MEPs voted against

A striking detail: Slovak MEPs, including those from the opposition, voted against the resolution. Representatives of the ruling coalition party Hlas-SD and the opposition KDH argued that freezing EU funds would hurt citizens, not the government. The dynamic is uncomfortable for the Parliament's majority. Even politicians who oppose Fico fear that conditionality punishes the wrong people — a concern the Centre for European Reform raised about the Hungary precedent, where €22 billion in frozen funds yielded limited, symbolic reforms before some were released as part of a political deal over Ukraine aid.

The resolution also demands that the Commission publish a complete list of final recipients of Recovery and Resilience Facility funding by 31 December 2026, or face legal action from Parliament. MEPs argue the Commission's interpretation of "final recipient" contradicts existing legislation and want harmonised, machine-readable disclosure across all instruments.

What the Commission does next is what matters

The Parliament's vote is a political signal, not a binding order. The Commission decides independently whether to trigger the conditionality mechanism. Any financial measures would require approval by a qualified majority of member states in the Council. EU Budget Commissioner Piotr Serafin personally encouraged Fico not to dismantle the Whistleblower Protection Office during a meeting in Bratislava in December 2025. Slovakia proceeded regardless. If the Commission does trigger the mechanism, Slovakia would have the opportunity to propose remedial measures before any funds are actually suspended — a process that could take months or years.

The question is not whether Slovakia's backsliding meets the legal threshold. It almost certainly does. The question is whether the EU is willing to use the tool it built for exactly this scenario — and whether, this time, it can avoid the pattern that turned Hungary's €22 billion freeze into a bargaining chip rather than a corrective.
