The 80% Versus the State: Georgia's Fight for Europe

10 May 2026 3 min read

On 28 November 2024, Georgian Prime Minister Irakli Kobakhidze announced the suspension of EU accession talks until 2028. Within hours, thousands of Georgians carrying EU flags filled Rustaveli Boulevard in Tbilisi. Police deployed water cannon and tear gas. Dozens were arrested. The protests have not stopped since — nearly 500 days later, they continue.

Georgia's constitutional commitment to EU membership is unambiguous. Enshrined in Article 78, it obliges state institutions to advance European integration. Yet the government elected to uphold that constitution has systematically dismantled the institutions that make membership possible. Eighty percent of Georgians support EU accession. Their government does not. That is the crisis — not a diplomatic disagreement, but a structural rupture between popular will and state power.

The machinery of repression

Georgian Dream's legislative programme since 2024 reads as a manual for authoritarian consolidation. The Transparency of Foreign Influence law, passed in May 2024, requires organisations receiving more than 20% foreign funding to register as carrying out the interests of a foreign power. The Foreign Agents Registration Act, which entered force on 31 May 2025, goes further — imposing punitive reporting requirements and enabling asset seizures. Both laws mirror Russia's 2012 foreign agents legislation almost verbatim.

The enforcement has been systematic. Between April 2024 and August 2025, at least 76 people faced criminal prosecution for protest-related activity; over 60 were imprisoned. Journalist Mzia Amaglobeli has been detained for more than 15 months on charges widely regarded as politically motivated. Surveillance cameras with facial recognition automate the fining of protesters — one civics teacher, Gota Chanturia, accrued $102,000 in penalties. New protest laws extend administrative detention from 5 to 60 days. The ECPMF documented 319 media freedom violations affecting 555 journalists since December 2023. Independent broadcasters face coordinated lawsuits. Journalists' bank accounts have been frozen.

A constitutional crisis, not a policy dispute

The dispute is not about the pace of accession. It is about whether the Georgian state remains bound by its own constitution. When all four opposition parties boycotted the October 2024 parliamentary elections — deemed neither free nor fair by the European Parliament — Georgian Dream became the sole represented force. In December, Mikheil Kavelashvili, a far-right GD loyalist, was installed as president, replacing the pro-European Salome Zourabichvili. The government then closed the Ministry of Foreign Affairs' Information Centre on NATO and EU integration — the institutional infrastructure of Georgia's European path, shuttered by the state itself.

Former President Zourabichvili has warned that Georgia risks becoming "the primary vector for Russian influence in the South Caucasus." Georgian Dream has refused to join Western sanctions on Russia. Bilateral trade between Russia and Georgia has surged since 2022. The Kulevi and Anaklia deep-sea port projects raise questions about Russian and Chinese strategic infrastructure footholds on the Black Sea coast.

Europe's institutional gap

The EU has responded, but within the limits of its existing framework. Accession has been halted. Thirty million euros in defence ministry assistance has been frozen. In March 2026, visa-free travel for Georgian diplomats and officials was suspended. HR/VP Kaja Kallas stated plainly: "If the government attacks its own people, silences journalists, and curtails freedom, there are consequences." The European Parliament has called for personal sanctions against Georgian Dream officials.

What the EU has not developed is a mechanism for supporting the population of a candidate country whose government has abandoned the accession path. The institutional architecture assumes that candidate governments want to join. When they do not, the framework has no answer — only a suspension that punishes the state by freezing the aspirations of its citizens. The Brussels Institute for Geopolitics has described Europe as risking "becoming a passive observer to the authoritarian unravelling" of a country where 80% of the population still wants in.

The question is not whether Georgia's government has turned away from Europe. It has. The question is whether Europe's institutions can distinguish between a government and a people — and whether they will build the tools to act on that distinction before the distinction ceases to matter.
