The Hague 5% Pledge: Verification by Design, Compliance by Faith

06 May 2026 4 min read

On 24–25 June 2025, NATO's 32 heads of state and government adopted the Hague Investment Plan: a commitment to spend 5% of GDP on defence and security by 2035, split into 3.5% for core defence requirements and 1.5% for resilience and infrastructure. Secretary General Rutte called it a transformational step. Spain's Prime Minister Sánchez called it "unreasonable and counterproductive" and secured a formal opt-out before the resolution was finalised. Slovakia dismissed the targets as "absurd." The declaration passed by consensus — with a visible fracture.

The question is not whether European governments can reach 5%. It is whether the pledge's design allows anyone to verify that they have. The 1.5% resilience category covers cyber-defence, infrastructure hardening, and civil preparedness — expenditure many governments were already making. Nine months after the summit, NATO still has no agreed definition of what counts toward that category. The annual "credible path" plans carry no sanction for non-compliance. The Hague commitment replicates the architecture of the 2014 Wales 2% pledge: a political target, a decade-long deadline, and no enforcement beyond peer pressure. Wales took ten years and a war to approach compliance. The Hague asks for more than double that figure with the same institutional machinery.

The 3.5 + 1.5 formula is a political construct, not a spending plan

The Hague Summit Declaration divides the 5% commitment into two categories: 3.5% of GDP for core defence requirements, benchmarked against the NATO Defence Planning Process, and 1.5% for defence- and security-related spending covering infrastructure, cyber-defence, civil preparedness, and support for Ukraine. The 3.5% component has measurable criteria. The 1.5% component does not. Nine months after the summit, the Atlantic Council published an analysis titled "NATO needs to define the substance of its 1.5 percent pledge" — the resilience category still lacks agreed definitions or quantifiable targets. The NATO resilience dashboard introduced after the 2023 Vilnius Summit does not operationalise those targets, and no multilateral body exists to align national interpretations of what counts. Secretary General Rutte shaped the formula to make the headline figure politically achievable. It allows governments to relabel pre-existing expenditure on civil emergency planning and infrastructure hardening as progress toward the 5% goal. The architecture is deliberate. The ambiguity is structural.

The Hague pledge follows the Wales playbook — with the same enforcement vacuum

The 2014 Wales Summit pledged allies to spend 2% of GDP on defence within a decade. At the time, only a handful of allies met the target — estimates vary between three and five depending on methodology. By 2018, roughly a quarter of allies were at or near it. It took Russia's 2022 invasion of Ukraine to catalyse serious compliance: by 2024, a majority of the 32 allies reached 2%. Wales had no enforcement mechanism, no sanctions, and no annual reporting requirement. The Hague pledge adds two procedural differences: allies must submit annual "credible, incremental path" plans starting at the 2026 summit, and a collective progress review is planned for 2029. These are procedural additions, not enforcement mechanisms. The 2035 deadline mirrors the ten-year horizon of Wales. The precedent is clear: a political commitment without binding consequences required a major war on the continent to approach compliance. The Hague asks for 2.5 times more spending under the same institutional architecture.

Spain's opt-out and Italy's fiscal gap expose the alliance's fault lines

Spain's formal exemption from the 5% target was not a negotiated compromise within the framework — it was an exemption from it entirely. Because a single member's veto could block the summit resolution, Prime Minister Sánchez leveraged NATO's consensus rules to secure an opt-out. Slovakia's leader called the targets "absurd." The opt-out establishes a precedent: a member state can decline to participate in the alliance's most ambitious peacetime spending commitment without consequence. Meanwhile, Italy faces the widest fiscal gap among major EU economies — core defence spending at roughly 1.6% of GDP means tripling current levels to reach 5%. Germany sits below 2.5%, France near 2.1%. The EU's Stability and Growth Pact compounds the tension: Article 126(2) TFEU sets a 3% deficit ceiling and a 60% debt-to-GDP limit that directly conflict with the NATO target. President von der Leyen proposed activating the national escape clause in early 2025, and the Council of the EU formally activated it on 8 July 2025, allowing 15 member states to exceed deficit limits for defence spending. Italy did not qualify. The result is a two-tier system where some EU-NATO members receive fiscal relief and others do not — and no mechanism coordinates NATO's annual "credible path" plans with the SGP's medium-term fiscal frameworks.

The real test arrives when allies submit their first "credible path" plans at the 2026 summit. Those plans will face two separate reviews: NATO's assessment of whether the incremental path to 5% is plausible, and the Commission's assessment of whether the same trajectory complies with the Stability and Growth Pact. No mechanism exists to reconcile these evaluations. If the annual plans and the SGP's medium-term fiscal frameworks diverge — and for Italy, they almost certainly will — the question is which institutional logic prevails: the alliance's security imperative or the Union's fiscal discipline. The answer will determine whether the 5% pledge produces a decade of coordinated rearmament or a decade of parallel paperwork.
