Mandatory declaration, voluntary service

11 May 2026 4 min read

On 6 May 2026, the German Defence Ministry confirmed plans to impose €250 fines on 18-year-old men who fail to complete the Bundeswehr's mandatory service questionnaire. The announcement, first reported by Der Spiegel and confirmed by a ministry spokeswoman to dpa, follows the dispatch of roughly 206,000 questionnaires since January — of which about 57,000, or 28 per cent, remain unanswered despite repeat reminders.

The fines expose a structural contradiction at the heart of Germany's new military service model. Since 1 January 2026, every 18-year-old man is legally obliged to declare his availability for military service by completing a questionnaire covering health, education, and motivation. Service itself remains entirely voluntary. The state compels the declaration but not the commitment. And when the declaration does not come, it reaches for the fine.

Mandatory declaration, voluntary service

The new law, approved by the Bundestag and awaiting Bundesrat consent, replaced the old voluntary-only recruitment system with what the federal government calls a "modernised" framework. All men turning 18 receive a letter containing a digital questionnaire. Women receive the same form but face no obligation to respond. The questionnaire asks for personal details, health status, educational background, and whether the respondent is willing to serve. Men who do not respond receive reminders. Men who still do not respond now face a €250 administrative fine.

The Defence Ministry describes the sanction as a last resort. Wehrbeauftragter Henning Otte, the parliamentary commissioner for the armed forces, has stressed that fines should only follow "ausführliche Information und angemessene Fristen" — thorough information and reasonable deadlines. His position is that recruitment must work through transparency and attractiveness, not coercion.

Of the 206,000 questionnaires dispatched since January, roughly 149,000 were returned on time. The remaining 57,000 represent not a marginal shortfall but a significant share of the cohort. The Defence Ministry has not publicly broken down the reasons for non-response — whether it reflects passive disengagement, active refusal, practical obstacles such as outdated address records, or a deliberate political choice.

Data collection without force generation

Germany's system is deliberately hybrid. The legal obligation extends only to filling out the form. The ICDS, in a December 2025 analysis titled "Pretence or Preparedness?", framed the question directly: does a model that compels data collection but not service constitute preparedness, or does it merely generate the appearance of it?

The recruitment targets are demanding — 20,000 volunteers in 2026, rising to 38,000 by 2030. In 2025, 12,286 volunteers joined the Bundeswehr, a 16 per cent increase on the previous year. The gap between current intake and the 2030 target remains substantial. The questionnaire is meant to close it by giving the Bundeswehr a pool of declared willing candidates to contact directly. Whether that pool translates into actual volunteers remains untested at scale.

From July 2027, the obligation deepens: men born in 2008 or later will also be required to attend a medical examination. Compulsory military service itself would only reinstate in the event of an armed attack on Germany. The incremental expansion of state obligation — from questionnaire to medical check, with fines as enforcement — tracks a pattern in European defence policy since 2022: the gradual widening of what "voluntary" means in practice.

A European model without a European precedent

Germany is not alone in recalibrating its military service framework. Nine NATO states currently operate some form of conscription, including Denmark, Estonia, Finland, Latvia, Lithuania, Norway, Sweden, and Turkey. Croatia reinstates conscription in 2026. Each model differs. Sweden and Norway use a selective lottery — all eligible citizens are assessed, a subset is called. The Baltic states maintain universal male conscription with limited exemptions.

Germany's approach — mandatory declaration, voluntary service — has no direct equivalent among its allies. It sits between the Scandinavian selective model and the fully voluntary systems of states like Belgium and the Netherlands. The distinction matters for European defence coordination. NATO's force generation targets, reinforced at the 2023 Vilnius summit, require member states to demonstrate credible pathways to manpower. A model that produces declaration data but not soldiers complicates that calculus — the numbers look different on paper than they do in the barracks.

Domestically, the political fault lines are clear. CDU/CSU favours a lottery system for partial conscription, arguing that the voluntary framework will not deliver the numbers the Bundeswehr needs. The SPD argues for improving the attractiveness of military service rather than expanding obligation — better pay, improved barracks conditions, clearer career pathways. The Greens are divided between a pacifist wing that opposes any obligation and a realist wing that accepts the defence case. The Left Party opposes any form of mandatory participation. The €250 fine has sharpened these divisions precisely because it makes the coercion in a supposedly voluntary system visible.

The question the German model raises for European defence planning is whether administrative obligation can substitute for institutional appeal. The Bundeswehr now has better data on who might serve. Whether those declarations become soldiers — and whether fines produce compliance or resentment — will shape how other member states judge the hybrid model's viability.
