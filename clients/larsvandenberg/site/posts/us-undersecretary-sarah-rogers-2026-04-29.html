The Doctrine

29 April 2026 4 min read

On March 26, 2026, Donald Trump nominated Sarah B. Rogers — already the Under Secretary of State for Public Diplomacy — to lead the U.S. Agency for Global Media, parent of Voice of America. If confirmed, one person will control both the State Department's outreach to foreign publics and a government-funded broadcaster reaching 350 million people worldwide. The nomination barely made the front pages. It should have.

Rogers has spent the six months since taking office building something the United States has never fielded: a diplomatic doctrine that treats allied democracies as the problem. Her instrument is "free speech." Her targets are the EU Digital Services Act, the UK Online Safety Act, and every European government that enforces hate speech law. This is not provocation. It is institutional architecture.

The doctrine

The National Security Strategy, published in December 2025, introduced a phrase that now runs through every Rogers speech and social media post: "civilizational erasure." The document claims some European allies face "the real and more stark prospect of civilizational erasure" through immigration and the erosion of democratic principles. It states that "should present trends continue, the continent will be unrecognizable in 20 years or less." Rogers adopted the language wholesale. In February 2026, she called for "civilizational self-confidence" — a direct echo of the NSS framing, now spoken from a top-10 State Department position.

The policy infrastructure follows the rhetoric. Rogers has pledged funding for "free speech initiatives" in Europe. The Financial Times reported she met with right-wing opposition parties across the continent to "fund MAGA-aligned think-tanks and charities." A senior Reform UK member described her as having "a state department slush fund to get MAGA-style things going in various places." The exact dollar figure is disputed — Reuters reported $500 million, Politico's April profile refers to "hundreds of thousands of dollars." No recipient organisations have been named publicly. The gap between the two figures is itself worth attention.

Inverting the mandate

The Under Secretary of State for Public Diplomacy was created in 1999 to rebuild American soft power after the Cold War drawdown. Its purpose: strengthen relationships between the U.S. and foreign publics, projecting liberal democratic values through cultural programmes and broadcasting. Voice of America and its predecessor agencies were instruments of Cold War information warfare against the Soviet bloc.

Rogers is using the same institutional machinery to do the opposite. On December 13, 2025, she met AfD foreign policy spokesman Markus Frohnmaier in Washington. When pressed, she defended the meeting by saying AfD took an "anti-censorship stance." She dismissed Der Spiegel's reporting on Frohnmaier's documented ties to Russian strategy papers as "unsubstantiated" — a characterisation that mirrors the AfD's own position. She appeared on a panel with an adviser to Viktor Orbán. She met a senior member of Reform UK.

Each meeting aligned her with a party that the host country's own institutions classify as a challenge to democratic norms. Each time, Rogers framed the alignment as free speech advocacy. The USAGM nomination compounds the inversion: if confirmed, she would control both the diplomatic channel and the broadcast channel — deciding what gets said to European publics, and through which megaphone.

The European silence

Germany's Federal Office for the Protection of the Constitution designated AfD a "confirmed right-wing extremist" force in May 2025. Secretary of State Marco Rubio called the designation "tyranny in disguise." No European government has lodged a formal diplomatic protest against Rogers's activities. No EU institution has issued a coordinated response.

Academics have been more direct. Léonie de Jonge of the University of Tübingen described the administration's posture as having "a vested interest in strengthening anti-democratic movements abroad." Georgios Samaras of King's College London called it "culture export and power projection." Both frames are accurate. Both describe something European institutions have not yet named in official language.

Rogers has also attacked the regulatory architecture directly. At a Prosperity Institute event in London in December 2025, she listed claims she said were true but suppressed in Europe: "grooming gangs are predominantly Pakistani," "COVID vaccines don't entirely prevent transmission," "COVID leaked from a lab," and the contested assertion that there are "average differences in IQ between racial groups." Each claim was selected to challenge a specific European content moderation norm. The strategy targets the EU's regulatory authority over digital platforms — the Digital Services Act — as censorship, not governance.

A senior American diplomat, armed with funding and potentially a broadcasting network, is openly working with parties that European intelligence services classify as extremist, while attacking the regulatory frameworks those democracies have built. Europe's silence is not restraint. It is a gap that Rogers is counting on.
