A European Army Without a Legal Home

06 May 2026 5 min read

On 28 January 2026, EU Defence Commissioner Andrius Kubilius published speaking notes proposing a standing European force of 100,000 troops and a European Security Council of 10–12 permanent members to bypass unanimity in defence decisions. By February, the Commission had distanced itself from its own commissioner's proposal, with press reports indicating a European army was not on the agenda. Kubilius himself had said as much a year earlier: "We are not working on setting up a European army."

The contradiction is structural, not personal. Protocol No.7 to the Treaty of Lisbon states the treaty "does not provide for the creation of a European army." Article 31(4) TEU locks unanimity into CSDP decisions — and the passerelle clause cannot unlock it for military matters without full treaty change under Article 48 TEU. Moving away from unanimity requires unanimity itself. The same double-lock killed the European Defence Community in 1954. The question is not whether Kubilius is right about the threat — the assessment he presents is broadly shared within European defence circles. It is whether a proposal that requires treaty change to become legal, and that the Commission will not own, advances European defence or displaces attention from mechanisms that could actually work.

A proposal without a legal home

Kubilius's speaking notes are specific in ambition and vague in legal pathway. The 100,000-troop force would replace the US conventional contingent in Europe. The European Security Council — 10 to 12 permanent members, including the UK — would make rapid defence decisions without requiring unanimity. Kubilius cites Russian threat assessments and the US National Defence Strategy's pivot to the Indo-Pacific as the strategic drivers. He invokes a line of predecessors: Juncker in 2017, Macron in 2018, Merkel the same year. His own addition, per his speaking notes: "these statements have not had real world consequences." The Commission's response confirmed that pattern. Kubilius rebranded the concept as a "rapid reaction force" rather than a European army — a term he acknowledged "is rejected in many countries and even causes deep dissent." The semantic shift signals political awareness. It does not resolve the legal problem. Protocol No.7 to the Treaty of Lisbon is unambiguous: the treaty does not provide for a European army.

The unanimity double-lock

The institutional barrier is not a matter of political will. It is codified. Article 31 TEU requires unanimity for all CFSP and CSDP decisions. Article 31(3) offers a passerelle — the European Council can, by unanimous agreement, authorise qualified majority voting in specific CFSP areas. But Article 31(4) explicitly excludes decisions with military or defence implications. To move beyond unanimity on defence, the EU would need full treaty change under Article 48 TEU — which itself requires unanimity. Von der Leyen called for QMV on sanctions and human rights in her 2020 State of the Union address. The proposal did not advance. The European Magnitsky Act similarly could not overcome the unanimity requirement. If qualified majority voting has not advanced on sanctions, its extension to defence decisions remains structurally distant. The EPRS identifies the core obstacle as "the absence of a common strategic culture among EU Member States." Constructive abstention — allowing a state to sit out without vetoing — is a workaround, not a solution.

1954 and 2026: the same three walls

On 30 August 1954, the French National Assembly voted down the European Defence Community treaty through a procedural motion. The treaty never reached substantive debate. Three reasons: fear of surrendering national sovereignty over armed forces, sovereignist reflexes among pro-Europe deputies, and hostility to the pace of federal integration. Germany regained sovereignty and rearmament through the Western European Union and NATO anyway — gaining more than the EDC would have allowed. The same Assembly approved the WEU less than four months later, because it was intergovernmental, not supranational. The design of the institution mattered more than the ambition behind it. The parallels are direct. Sovereignty concerns persist — member states have not resolved who would hold command authority over a European force. The federal-intergovernmental tension persists — Bruegel's European Defence Mechanism was designed outside the treaties for exactly this reason. French reluctance to subordinate strategic autonomy persists. The EDC's ghost argues for the intergovernmental path. Bruegel's European Defence Mechanism, published April 2025, proposes a capital-based institution open to EU and non-EU democracies, requiring no treaty change. Spain has reportedly championed it in Council. It has traction. It has no spotlight.

The structural question is not whether European defence needs more integration — it does, and the threat assessment driving Kubilius's proposal is broadly shared. It is which integration path has a legal pathway and institutional traction. Bruegel's EDM requires no treaty change, welcomes the UK, and already has a member state pushing it in Council. Kubilius's force requires treaty change that Article 31(4) TEU makes structurally unlikely, and the Commission will not sponsor it. The 1954 precedent is instructive: the same states that rejected the supranational EDC approved the intergovernmental WEU four months later. The design of the institution mattered more than the ambition behind it. If Europe's defence ministers want a mechanism that can be built before the threat window closes, the intergovernmental path is the one that does not require unanimity to abolish unanimity.
