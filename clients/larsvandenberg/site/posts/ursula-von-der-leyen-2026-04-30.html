The veto demand

30 April 2026 3 min read

On April 27, Ursula von der Leyen faced her own party in a closed-door session in Berlin. The CDU/CSU parliamentary group had handed her a 27-point document — one demand per member state, the number chosen for effect — titled "Agenda for a sustainable reduction of bureaucracy at EU level." The flagship demand: a supervisory body with veto power over any new legislative proposal the Commission puts forward. Von der Leyen signalled readiness for change. She did not concede anything specific.

This is not a routine disagreement between allies. It is the most direct attempt by a member-state government to restructure the Commission's legislative initiative power in decades. And it comes from the chancellor of the EU's largest member state — von der Leyen's own party colleague in the European People's Party.

The veto demand

The CDU/CSU document targets the Commission's exclusive right of legislative initiative, enshrined in the EU treaties. The conservatives propose either creating a new supervisory body or empowering the existing Regulatory Scrutiny Board — currently an internal advisory panel — with a "fundamental veto right" over new proposals. Either route would likely require treaty change.

The remaining demands call for a "narrower interpretation" of EU institutions' powers and staff reductions across European structures. The cumulative effect is a challenge not just to specific rules but to the scope of the Commission's regulatory mandate. The right of initiative is the Commission's constitutional anchor. A veto body sitting above it would alter the EU's institutional architecture. The CDU/CSU knows this. The demand is designed to shift the Overton window — to make treaty change a live option rather than a theoretical one.

Shared diagnosis, diverging prescriptions

Both Merz and von der Leyen cite the Draghi report on EU competitiveness decline as grounds for action. The diagnosis is shared. The prescription is not. Merz wants the Commission constrained. Von der Leyen wants it to self-reform. That gap is now structural, not tactical.

Germany cut its 2026 growth forecast in half the week before the ultimatum. The Iran war's economic fallout and persistent energy-price volatility have intensified pressure on Merz to deliver on election promises. His coalition with the SPD limits how far he can push domestic deregulation. Brussels is the more visible target.

Von der Leyen's position is weaker than it appears. She has survived four no-confidence votes since beginning her second term — the most faced by any Commission president in over a decade. Prediction markets still price her survival through 2026 at 82 percent. But the source of this ultimatum matters more than its content. Merz is not an opposition leader testing boundaries. He is a chancellor with a mandate, pushing the Commission president who came from his own party.

The precedent problem

If a German chancellor can extract concessions via ultimatum, what prevents Paris, Warsaw, or Rome from trying the same? The Commission's institutional independence depends on no single member state being able to dictate terms. Merz's move tests that principle directly.

Von der Leyen's vague commitment to "change" — making Europe "faster and more efficient" so companies can "regain competitiveness" — is a holding pattern, not a strategy. The question is whether it becomes a negotiation over which of the 27 demands survive, or whether she treats the ultimatum as the opening bid it likely is. The answer will shape how the Commission defends its institutional prerogatives long after this particular clash fades from the headlines.
