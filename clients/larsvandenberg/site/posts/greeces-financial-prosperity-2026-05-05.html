Greece's Financial Prosperity

05 May 2026 4 min read

In March 2025, Moody's upgraded Greece to Baa3, completing the country's return to investment grade across all three major credit agencies — S&P, Fitch, and Moody's. The debt-to-GDP ratio fell from 209.4% in 2020 to 146.1% in 2025, among the sharpest reductions in the euro area. Greece posted a primary surplus for a third consecutive year — reported at 4.9% of GDP — while most of its peers ran deficits. Finance Minister Hatzidakis secured ECOFIN approval for revised Recovery Plan parameters in 2026, with absorption targets set at 64%. The trajectory is one of institutional rehabilitation: from troika-supervised programme country to compliant member state under standard European Semester governance.

Fiscal compliance does not equal lived convergence. RRF-funded investment drives current growth, but the facility expires in August 2026, and projections taper GDP to 1.7% by 2027. Bank profits exceeded €4.6 billion in 2025. Sixty per cent of private-sector workers earn €1,200 or less. Youth unemployment, depending on the measure, ranges from roughly 21% to 25%. The Commission flagged Greece for macroeconomic imbalances even as it praised its budget. The bailout era ended. The convergence gap has not.

From programme country to compliant member

Greece's transition from corrective enforcement to routine coordination followed a sequence of institutional decisions that progressively restored fiscal sovereignty. The ESM programme expired on 20 August 2018, ending eight years of troika-supervised conditionality under Article 136 TFEU. Post-programme monitoring continued — as it does until 75% of official loans are repaid — but Greece regained budgetary control. The excessive deficit procedure, opened under Article 126 TFEU in 2009, closed in September 2017. By November 2025, Economics Commissioner Valdis Dombrovskis presented Greece as one of twelve eurozone members "fully compliant" with ECOFIN's fiscal recommendations. The 2026 draft budget passed the Commission's assessment without reservation. Greece is not among the nine member states currently under excessive deficit procedure. ECOFIN endorsed Greece's medium-term fiscal-structural plan in January 2025 under Regulation 2024/1263, setting a net expenditure path through 2028. The institutional language has shifted. What was corrective is now coordinative.

The long arc of conditionality

Greece's current position is legible only against the architecture that produced it. Three successive programmes channelled €289 billion through the EFSF and ESM between 2010 and 2018, each with quarterly compliance reviews, structural reform requirements, and privatisation targets. The troika — European Commission, ECB, IMF — exercised direct budgetary oversight. GDP contracted by roughly 25%. Unemployment exceeded 27%. By some estimates, over 400,000 people left during the crisis years, though broader measures of emigration from 2010 to 2022 put the figure substantially higher. The reformed fiscal framework that now governs Greece replaced that scaffolding with medium-term plans, net expenditure paths, and European Semester reviews. The mechanisms are less coercive. The debt remains: 146.1% of GDP in 2025, with government targets reportedly aiming for 133.4% by 2028. Post-programme monitoring is still active. Greece's fiscal sovereignty is real but incomplete — exercised within parameters set by the same institutional architecture that once administered austerity.

Quiet convergence, persistent divergence

The member-state politics of Greece's recovery are notably less polarised than the North-South disputes of 2010–2015. No government has publicly challenged Greece's fiscal trajectory. The frugals acknowledge compliance. But quiet consensus should not be mistaken for structural alignment. The Commission flagged Greece among seven member states with potential macroeconomic imbalances in its autumn 2025 alert under the Macroeconomic Imbalance Procedure — bank NPLs, current account deficits, and investment gaps remain. The domestic picture is starker. Bank profits above €4.6 billion in 2025 against a median private-sector wage of €1,200 or less. Youth unemployment ranging from roughly 21% to 25% depending on measure. RRF absorption at only around 28% of milestones with the August 2026 deadline approaching. The institutional verdict is positive. The lived economy diverges.

The RRF facility expires in August 2026. When it does, the growth model that fiscal compliance has so far allowed Greece to defer confronting will face its structural test. The government's own projections taper to 1.7% by 2027. Private investment has not scaled. The banking oligopoly still extracts more from depositors than it lends to SMEs. The Commission's MIP alert is not a footnote — it is the institutional shorthand for a country whose balance sheet has healed while its productive structure has not. Greece has proved it can comply. The next test is whether it can converge.
