EU Federalisation and the Populist Loop

11 May 2026 3 min read

When European Parliament results landed on 9 June 2024, far-right parties held 187 of 720 seats — approximately 26% of the chamber, up from roughly 15% in 2014. Marine Le Pen's National Rally more than doubled the vote of Macron's centrist coalition in France. Germany's AfD polled second. Austria's Freedom Party approached 30%. Portugal's Chega grew from 12 MPs to 49 in two years. The European Council responded by reappointing Ursula von der Leyen for a second term via a coalition of EPP, S&D, and Renew Europe — 401 votes out of 720 — while maintaining the cordon sanitaire around the three far-right groups. No treaty change, no democratic reform mechanism, and no adjustment to the Spitzenkandidaten process was proposed.

The pattern is structural, not episodic. Each cycle of deeper integration without corresponding democratic consent mechanisms widens the legitimacy gap that populist movements exploit. The Euro lauch and subsequent Eurozone crisis converted ECB-imposed austerity into governing mandates for Syriza, Podemos, and the AfD. The Maastricht and Lisbon treaty referenda — rejected by Danish voters in 1992, by French voters in 2005, and by Irish voters in 2008, only to be rerun or bypassed — established a precedent. Democratic rejection is treated as a procedural obstacle, not a verdict. The 2024 results confirm the feedback loop.

The 2024 elections signal a shift in chamber composition

The 2024 European Parliament elections delivered a measurable shift in the balance of power. Across the EU, far-right parties secured approximately 26% of seats — 187 out of 720 — up from roughly 15% in 2014. In France, Le Pen's National Rally more than doubled the result of Macron's Renaissance list. In Germany, the AfD polled second, level with the SPD. Austria's Freedom Party neared 30%. Portugal's Chega quadrupled its parliamentary presence in two years. Italy's Brothers of Italy, tracing its lineage to a post-fascist movement, now sits in government as a mainstream conservative force. The European Council responded by reappointing von der Leyen on a coalition of EPP, S&D, and Renew — 401 votes — and excluding the Patriots for Europe, ECR, and Europe of Sovereign Nations from governing arrangements. The Spitzenkandidaten process, partially revived when von der Leyen ran as the EPP's lead candidate, remained a Council-driven appointment in practice. No institutional reform followed. The status quo remains.

A documented causal chain links integration to backlash

The tension between integration and democratic consent has operated across three decades. The 1992 Maastricht Treaty — creating the EU and the euro — passed France with 51% and was initially rejected by Denmark. The 2005 French and Dutch referenda killed the Constitutional Treaty outright. The 2008 Irish rejection of Lisbon was overturned in a second vote after concessions. Each rejection was followed by a rerun or a bypass. During the Eurozone crisis, states that had surrendered monetary autonomy faced ECB-enforced austerity with no domestic veto. Syriza rose from a coalition junior to a governing party on anti-austerity anger. The AfD, founded in 2013 as an anti-bailout party, pivoted to broader populism. Italy's Five Star Movement channelled the same governance distance into electoral mobilisation. The CER identifies a vicious circle. Governance failures fuel populist gains; populist gains block the reforms that would reduce the deficit; paralysis feeds the next wave.

Member state fault lines reflect divergent structural grievances

The integration-backlash dynamic unfolds differently across member states, though the structural mechanism remains consistent. In creditor states — Germany, the Netherlands, Finland — populist parties exploit bailout fatigue and sovereignty loss. In debtor states — Greece, Italy, Portugal — they exploit austerity imposition and democratic distance from Frankfurt. The 2024 results compress both dynamics. Le Pen in France and the AfD in Germany draw from sovereignty anxiety. Chega in Portugal and Meloni in Italy draw from governance resentment. The Journal of European Integration (2025) notes that populist critique of the EU is not exclusively far-right. Left-wing and non-nativist movements articulate the same democratic deficit grievance. The counter-evidence matters. Ireland's repeated referenda demonstrate that integration can proceed with democratic legitimacy when citizens hold meaningful veto power. The variable is consent architecture, not integration depth.

The persistence of this loop suggests that the EU's current institutional framework cannot reconcile deeper integration with the demand for national democratic oversight. The question is whether the Union can develop a consent mechanism that functions as a genuine veto rather than a procedural hurdle.
