Landsbergis at the Atlantic Council

07 May 2026 3 min read

On 6 May 2026, the Atlantic Council appointed Gabrielius Landsbergis — Lithuania's foreign minister from December 2020 to November 2024 — as co-chair of its Reimagining European Defense and Innovation Task Force. He joins Gen (Ret) Christopher Cavoli, former SACEUR and current Atlantic Council board member, who chaired the task force from its launch on 14 January 2026. Landsbergis will operate from Washington, not Brussels, as a distinguished fellow at the Scowcroft Center for Strategy and Security.

The appointment is a structural signal. The task force's remit — European defence industrial capacity, innovation, and transatlantic burden-sharing — sits within the policy space of Article 42 TEU, the European Defence Agency, and the Commission's defence-industrial strategy. Yet the work is hosted by a Washington institution, co-chaired by a former EU foreign minister and a former NATO supreme commander. The question is not whether the Atlantic Council can run such a task force. It is whether the EU's own institutional architecture can generate the same policy ambition — or whether it will keep outsourcing that thinking.

Two co-chairs, two institutional logics

The task force operates under the Scowcroft Center's Transatlantic Security Initiative. Its mandate: "actionable recommendations" on European defence industrial capacity and US-Europe collaboration. Cavoli, SACEUR until his retirement, brings deep knowledge of NATO command structures and US military perspectives on European capability gaps. He is also a partner at Valor Equity Partners — positioning the task force at the intersection of defence policy and private capital. Landsbergis brings a different register: four years as foreign minister of a Baltic state on Russia's border, during which he pushed for harder sanctions on Moscow and accelerated military support for Ukraine. His appointment adds a vocal European policy voice to what might otherwise read as a US-driven exercise.

The mandate exists. The speed does not.

The task force's subject matter overlaps with work already underway in Brussels. The EDA runs capability development plans under its CDP cycle. The Commission published its Defence Readiness Roadmap 2030 in late 2025. The Council has held informal sessions on defence financing and procurement reform. None have produced the sharp, output-oriented recommendations the Atlantic Council task force promises. The EU institutional machinery — designed for unanimity among 27 member states under Article 16(4) TEU — is structurally slower than a think tank that can convene experts, commission papers, and publish within months. That speed gap is not new. What is new is that a former EU foreign minister has chosen to exercise influence from outside the institutional framework rather than within it. Landsbergis could have taken a role at an EU-affiliated body — the EEAS, the EDA, a special representative post. He chose Washington.

Strategic autonomy, advanced from Washington

Landsbergis's own policy positions sharpen the appointment's significance. In a 2025 interview, he argued that Europe must stop waiting for Washington decisions on defence and energy, reduce its dependence on the US, and set clear NATO red lines. The tension is deliberate: a politician who calls for European strategic autonomy now co-chairs a task force hosted by a Washington institution, staffed substantially by American defence professionals, directed at influencing transatlantic policy from the US side of the Atlantic. If EU institutions are not yet producing the policy architecture Landsbergis wants, the Atlantic Council offers a platform that can. Whether that platform's recommendations feed back into EU institutional processes — or simply circulate within the transatlantic policy community — remains an open structural question.

The task force has published no member list, no timeline for deliverables, no defined output format beyond "actionable recommendations." Its influence will depend on whether those recommendations reach the right desks in Brussels — and whether the Commission and Council treat a Washington-produced policy document as a serious input into EU defence planning.
