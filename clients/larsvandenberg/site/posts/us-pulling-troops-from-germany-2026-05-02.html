The Troop Withdrawal Europe Should Have Asked For

2 May 2026 3 min read

On 1 May 2026, Defence Secretary Pete Hegseth ordered the withdrawal of approximately 5,000 US troops from Germany, with a completion window of six to twelve months. The order came two days after President Trump publicly floated the idea on social media — and one day after Pentagon officials told Politico they had not been briefed beforehand. The drawdown returns US troop levels in Germany to roughly 33,000, undoing the Biden-era augmentation deployed after Russia's 2022 invasion of Ukraine.

Washington frames the withdrawal as a posture adjustment. The timing tells a different story. Chancellor Friedrich Merz had criticised the US-Iran war three days earlier, calling it "humiliating" for the United States and questioning its exit strategy. Trump's response — telling Merz to "fix his broken country" and accusing him of wanting Iran to acquire nuclear weapons — made the retaliatory character explicit. A senior Pentagon official described Germany's rhetoric as "unhelpful." Alliance commitments are now contingent on political alignment with the White House.

A politically driven drawdown

The Pentagon's own global posture review did not recommend a major European withdrawal. Trump's 30 April post pre-empted the institutional process. Officials scrambled to produce an order matching the president's announcement. The 5,000-figure reverses the specific augmentation deployed after February 2022 — a politically convenient baseline, not a strategic assessment. Separately, the Pentagon decided not to replace an Army brigade rotating out of Romania, raising concerns about deterrence on NATO's eastern flank. Trump also said he would "probably" consider withdrawing troops from Italy and Spain. Troop presence is being used as leverage, not calibrated to threat.

The institutional architecture already exists

The EU spent the past year building the financial and legislative framework for strategic autonomy. The Readiness 2030 plan, launched in March 2025, targets over €800 billion in defence spending by 2030. Its centrepiece is the €150 billion SAFE loan instrument for joint procurement — the first EU-level mechanism designed to aggregate demand across member states rather than replicate national shopping lists. The legal basis exists. The funding instruments exist. What lacks is political urgency. Every previous attempt to move European defence integration from declaration to delivery — from Permanent Structured Cooperation to the European Defence Fund — stalled on the same problem: member states could always assume the American backstop. That assumption is now a liability.

The withdrawal exposes divergent threat perceptions

Poland and the Baltic states are focused on the eastern flank, where the decision not to replace the Romania brigade matters more than the Germany drawdown. Paris has long argued for European strategic autonomy and will read this as vindication. Berlin faces the hardest calculus: the Bundeswehr remains under-strength despite increased spending, and Germany hosts the largest US logistical infrastructure in Europe. Losing 5,000 troops does not cripple that footprint, but the signal does. The coalition in Berlin must now decide whether to accelerate national procurement — which competes with the EU-level approach — or to channel spending through joint mechanisms like SAFE. The choice tracks an older fault line: sovereignty through independence versus sovereignty through coordination.

The frameworks are in place. The money is allocated. The political window is open — and will not stay open indefinitely. Whether this moment, delivered as punishment for dissent rather than conceived as strategic recalibration, finally breaks the habit of treating American troop deployments as permanent infrastructure depends entirely on what Berlin, Paris, and Warsaw do in the next budget cycle.
