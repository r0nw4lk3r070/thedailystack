The Pact That Preserves What It Promised to Replace

06 May 2026 4 min read

On 12 June 2026, the EU Pact on Migration and Asylum enters full application. Regulation (EU) 2024/1348 — the Asylum and Migration Management Regulation — formally replaces Dublin III. New border procedures, screening rules, and a solidarity pool activate the same day. The date is fixed. The architecture is not.

In August 2025, the European Court of Justice struck down Italy's "safe country of origin" list. The ruling: member states cannot designate a country as safe unless that safety covers the entire population, including groups at risk of persecution. Italy's law allowed exceptions for vulnerable groups. The court rejected that approach outright. The judgment dismantles the legal basis for offshore processing under the Italy-Albania protocol. It also casts doubt on the accelerated procedures the Pact extends EU-wide.

In December 2025, the Danish presidency brokered Council Implementing Decision (EU) 2025/2642: a solidarity pool of 21,000 relocations or €420 million in financial contributions. Poland secured a full exemption. Hungary refuses to participate at all. The Commission's own June 2025 implementation report flagged gaps in Eurodac deployment, border screening infrastructure, and reception capacity. EUAA data indicates asylum claims dropped in early 2025.

The Pact was sold as a structural fix for thirty years of Dublin dysfunction. It is not. The AMMR preserves the first-country-of-entry principle. The solidarity pool lets wealthy states buy their way out. The ECJ has already invalidated the safe-country shortcut the Pact relies on for accelerated procedures. The Commission's own assessment confirms operational readiness lags behind legislative ambition. The numbers the Pact was designed to manage are declining. What remains is a framework that looks like reform, manages the optics of crisis, and reproduces the structural asymmetry it claimed to resolve.

A framework without a foundation

The Pact's central legal pillar — the safe-country-of-origin designation, which triggers accelerated border procedures — was hollowed out on 1 August 2025. The ECJ ruled that member states cannot classify a country as "safe" unless that safety extends to the entire population, including groups at risk of persecution. Italy's law permitted exceptions for vulnerable groups. The court said no. That ruling dismantles the legal basis for the Italy-Albania protocol, under which asylum seekers from designated safe countries were to be processed in Italian-funded detention centres on Albanian territory.

The Pact extends the safe-country concept EU-wide. Under the ECJ's stricter standard, member states will face far more rigorous individual assessments than the accelerated procedures assumed. This is precisely the procedural shortcut the Pact needed to function at scale. Without it, the entire fast-track mechanism stalls.

The Commission's June 2025 implementation report, published halfway through the two-year transition, confirms the operational architecture is not ready. Eurodac, the biometric database underpinning the system, has made progress at EU level — but several member states lag on national deployment. Border screening locations remain unidentified. Reception capacity is not guaranteed. The report acknowledges that "sufficient capacity and adequate conditions are not yet guaranteed across the Union." That is a candid admission. The Pact's core objective — preventing secondary movements — faces unresolved operational barriers.

Thirty years of the same asymmetry

The Pact was presented as a rupture with Dublin. It is not. The Dublin system, from the 1990 Dublin Convention through Dublin III (Regulation 604/2013), assigned asylum responsibility to the member state of first entry. Greece, Italy, Spain, and Cyprus carried the load. Inland states looked away.

The AMMR formally repeals Dublin III on 1 July 2026 and introduces a solidarity mechanism. Substantively, as Prof. Francesco Maiani argues, the core responsibility criteria are unchanged. The first-country-of-entry principle still dominates. Art. 37(4) AMMR tightens cessation rules and streamlines timelines — but these are modifications, not a redesign. The solidarity pool — voluntary, with a financial buyout option — reproduces Dublin's structural imbalance. Frontline states still process the claims. Inland states still choose whether to share the burden.

The fault lines

The solidarity pool crystallises the divide. Agreed under the Danish presidency in December 2025, it sets a target of 21,000 relocations or €420 million in contributions. Poland secured a full exemption. Hungary refuses to participate at all. Cyprus, Greece, Italy, and Spain are designated recipients. The pool's design guarantees that the states most opposed to sharing responsibility can pay instead of relocating — or simply walk away.

Meanwhile, EUAA data for the first half of 2025 indicates asylum claims in EU+ countries fell to approximately 400,000, with recognition rates at historically low levels. The crisis the Pact was framed to address is receding. The structural asymmetry it was meant to resolve remains intact.

The Pact will enter force on schedule. What it will not do is redistribute responsibility. The ECJ has already removed one pillar. The solidarity pool lets dissenting states opt out of another. The operational infrastructure is incomplete. The migratory pressures the framework was designed to address are diminishing. The question for the next cycle is whether the EU is willing to confront the structural asymmetry the AMMR inherited from Dublin — or whether it will continue to legislate around it, passing regulations that look like reform while leaving the underlying distribution of responsibility untouched.
