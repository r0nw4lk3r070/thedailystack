Macron Builds Europe's Security Architecture on Three Tracks

27 April 2026 4 min read

On 20 April 2026, Emmanuel Macron and Polish Prime Minister Donald Tusk signed a joint declaration at the first bilateral summit under the Treaty of Nancy, committing to nuclear deterrence cooperation, integrated air and missile defence, and a bilateral defence plan running through 2028. Five days later, Macron stood in Athens alongside Greek PM Kyriakos Mitsotakis and declared the EU's mutual assistance clause "stronger" than NATO's Article 5. Three days before Nancy, he had co-chaired a Paris summit with UK Prime Minister Keir Starmer to form a multinational maritime coalition for the Strait of Hormuz — explicitly excluding the United States.

Three summits, three tracks, one claim: Europe can handle its own security without Washington. The financial credibility comes from France's €36 billion rearmament programme. The institutional credibility is what Macron is trying to build. Whether the architecture holds depends on whether diplomatic declarations become operational capacity before the political window closes.

The Nuclear Track: Nancy Without the Warhead

The Treaty of Nancy declaration covers nuclear deterrence cooperation, integrated air and missile defence, Earth and space observation, and a bilateral defence plan for 2026–2028. Macron told the press conference that "all components" of nuclear deterrence are included. Tusk confirmed Poland was negotiating an "advanced nuclear deterrence programme" with France and European allies. Reports indicate joint nuclear exercises near the Russian border are being planned.

The treaty text does not mention nuclear weapons. What "nuclear deterrence cooperation" means operationally — joint exercises, hosting of French nuclear forces on Polish territory, or a diplomatic framework without operational consequences — remains unspecified. Macron's press conference statement is a political commitment, not a treaty provision. The distinction matters. Poland borders Russia's Kaliningrad exclave and Belarus and feels Russian nuclear threats directly. Hosting French nuclear weapons on Polish soil would escalate tensions with Moscow in ways Warsaw may not want. Within NATO, a bilateral nuclear agreement outside the alliance framework raises questions about cohesion. The current wording accommodates both interpretations. That is likely deliberate.

The Institutional Track: Article 42.7 vs Article 5

In Athens on 25 April, Macron called the EU's mutual assistance clause — Article 42.7 of the Treaty on European Union — "unambiguous" and "stronger" than NATO's Article 5. France and Greece signed nine bilateral agreements, including an extended defence pact.

The claim is striking. Article 5 of the North Atlantic Treaty obliges collective defence, with the United States as the primary military guarantor. Article 42.7 of the EU Treaty speaks of "aid and assistance by all the means in their power" but leaves room for interpretation about what that aid entails. Macron pointed to coordinated military aid to Cyprus as proof the clause works. That is a relevant precedent. It is also a precedent of crisis response, not of collective defence against a military attack.

Europe does not need to wait for America to organise solidarity. That is the message. The question is whether the mechanisms exist to convert solidarity into military reality. France and Greece now have a bilateral pact. That is concrete. It is also bilateral, not European.

The Maritime Track: Hormuz Without Washington

On 17 April, Macron and Starmer co-chaired the Strait of Hormuz Maritime Freedom of Navigation Initiative summit in Paris. Dozens of nations signed on to deploy a multinational maritime force "as soon as conditions allow." Iran has blocked the strait since February 2026, following US-Israeli air strikes that eliminated Iranian leadership. The United States is explicitly not part of the initiative.

This is the third track on which Macron demonstrates European autonomy — this time well beyond continental borders. The logic is consistent: if Europe can act in the Strait of Hormuz without the US, it can also defend its own continent without the US. But the gap between theory and practice is wide. A coalition that has not yet formed has no forces, no logistics chain, no operational command. The initiative still needs to prove it can escort ships through a strait controlled by Iran — with or without the US Navy.

What is missing across all three tracks is specificity. The nuclear track has no operational agreements — only a declaration and a press conference. The EU track has no tested mechanisms — only a bilateral pact and a rhetorical claim. The Hormuz track rests on a coalition whose military credibility remains unproven. Meanwhile, the Franco-German-Spanish FCAS fighter jet programme hangs by a thread. Macron called it "absolutely not dead" after talks with Chancellor Merz, but Dassault and Airbus remain deadlocked over work share and intellectual property, and mediators have requested ten more days to salvage the programme. If Europe cannot build a joint fighter jet, the question of sustaining a joint security architecture looms larger.

The €36 billion rearmament gives Macron credibility. Credibility is not capacity. Three tracks simultaneously demand more diplomatic bandwidth and military resources than France currently possesses. Macron is building the architecture. Whether the structure holds depends on what fills it — and whether the foundations bear weight when the wind shifts.
