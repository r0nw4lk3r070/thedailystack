The Summit Problem

30 April 2026 4 min read

On April 27, six NATO sources — a senior European official and five diplomats — told Reuters the alliance is considering whether to stop holding annual leaders' summits. The 2027 summit in Albania would likely proceed, probably in autumn. But 2028, Donald Trump's final full calendar year in office, may pass without one at all. The decision rests with Secretary General Mark Rutte. Nothing has been decided yet.

This is not a scheduling adjustment. NATO is debating whether to restructure the rhythm of its highest-level diplomacy to reduce the risk of public confrontation with an American president who has made the alliance a recurring target. The question underneath is blunt: can a 32-member alliance function normally when its summit calendar is shaped around managing one head of government?

The debate inside NATO

The Reuters report, drawn from six sources speaking on condition of anonymity, describes a deliberate internal conversation. Some member states want summits every two years rather than annually. The 2026 Ankara summit on July 7–8 is confirmed. Albania in 2027 is likely. The gap would come in 2028 — the year that makes the calculus plain. Skipping a summit during a US election year is framed as damage limitation: avoiding a repeat of summits where Trump's public confrontations with allies dominated coverage and eroded the appearance of cohesion.

The calculation is straightforward. A 2028 summit with Trump on the campaign trail risks becoming a platform for demands and denunciations rather than collective decisions. Better, some argue, to let the year pass without that stage. The alternative — holding a summit and hoping for decorum — has recent precedent for why it fails. At The Hague in June 2025, Trump pressured allies into a 5% of GDP defence spending target. The Heritage Foundation called the summit "a huge success." Mark Rutte declared it the foundation for a "stronger, fairer and more lethal Alliance." The headline was unity. The subtext was concession. The concern now is that a 2028 summit would repeat that pattern on a larger, more politically charged stage.

From Cold War rarity to annual routine

NATO summits have not always been annual. During the Cold War, from 1949 to 1991, the alliance held roughly 12 leaders' summits — one every three to four years. They were reserved for major strategic decisions. Routine business moved through ministerial meetings and diplomatic channels.

Frequency increased after the Cold War as NATO expanded eastward and took on out-of-area operations. But the shift to annual summits was never formally codified. It became practice under Jens Stoltenberg, driven by the pace of events: the Ukraine war, defence spending disputes, Finland and Sweden's accession. Since 2021, leaders have met every summer — Brussels, Madrid, Vilnius, Washington, The Hague. The current debate is whether the political conditions that made annual summits productive still hold. Or whether they have become a liability.

What the allies actually disagree on

The fault lines are structural. Eastern flank members — Poland, the Baltic states — have the strongest case for frequent leader-level contact. For them, Russia's war in Ukraine is not a periodic agenda item; it is a daily reality. Annual summits provide a mechanism for commitments on defence spending, force posture, and Ukraine's security guarantees. Fewer summits could mean fewer opportunities to advance Kyiv's NATO trajectory and lock in allied commitments.

Germany and France may see the case differently. Both face domestic pressure on defence budgets after the 5% GDP target agreed at The Hague. A two-year cycle reduces the frequency of politically costly spending conversations at the leader level. It also reduces the risk of public friction with Washington that complicates their own political positions. The calculation is different in Warsaw than in Berlin.

Then there is the signal problem. Discussing the change at all sends a message: NATO is willing to restructure its decision-making rhythm to avoid friction with one president. Critics call it self-censorship. Proponents call it pragmatism. The distinction matters less than the fact that the deliberations — reported by Reuters from six sources — are now public. The leak itself is a form of pressure on Rutte, whose decision on 2028 will be the first major structural choice of his tenure.

The precedent risk is real. If NATO adjusts its summit calendar around Trump, future leaders may expect similar accommodation — not just American presidents, but any leader willing to make summits contentious enough that avoidance becomes the preferred strategy. An alliance that restructures itself to sidestep its most disruptive member has changed in kind, not just in schedule. Whether that is adaptation or erosion is the question Rutte now has to answer — and the answer will shape how NATO conducts its business long after Trump leaves office.
