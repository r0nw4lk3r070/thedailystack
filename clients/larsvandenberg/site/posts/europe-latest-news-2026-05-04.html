Defence Sovereignty Commands Capital. Tech Sovereignty Commands Regulation.

04 May 2026 4 min read

On 20 April 2026, Siemens CEO Roland Busch confirmed that the majority of the company's €1 billion industrial AI investment will flow to the United States, citing Europe's regulatory burden as the deciding factor. Days later, the Digital Omnibus trilogue collapsed after twelve hours of Council-Parliament negotiation over a single unresolved question — the overlap between AI Act high-risk rules and sectoral product-safety regulation. Meanwhile, the Commission's September 2026 evaluation of the Chips Act is expected to confirm what SEMI Europe's April position paper already states: €69 billion in catalysed investment has not moved Europe's global semiconductor market share beyond ten per cent, half the 2030 target.

The asymmetry is structural. Defence sovereignty — the ReArm Europe initiative, the €150 billion SAFE loan facility under Article 212 TFEU, Germany's expanded defence spending — commands capital at scale. Tech sovereignty — the Chips Act, the AI Act, the Data Act — commands regulatory architecture instead. Over one hundred tech-focused laws and two hundred and seventy regulators now operate across the digital policy space, according to BusinessEurope. The Draghi report's warning that the EU's regulatory stance towards tech companies hampers innovation has not yet shifted the balance. Capital follows the path of least regulatory resistance. That path currently leads away from Brussels.

Two sovereign tracks, one budget

The Commission's 2026 budget, adopted under Article 312 TFEU, allocates €4.6 billion to competitiveness and €6.2 billion to security and defence — a ratio that reflects political priorities rather than the Draghi report's call for €800 billion in additional annual investment. Siemens' decision to redirect its AI spending followed Busch's public assessment that compliance costs across the AI Act, the Data Act, and the Cyber Resilience Act made European deployment financially uncompetitive. The Digital Omnibus trilogue, designed to simplify and consolidate these rules, stalled after the Council and Parliament could not agree on whether AI Act high-risk classifications should override sectoral product-safety rules or run parallel. DLA Piper's analysis of the impasse notes that the proposed deferral of compliance deadlines to December 2027 leaves companies navigating two overlapping timelines. The Chips Act tells a parallel story. SEMI Europe's April position paper documents €69 billion in catalysed private and public investment, but Europe's global semiconductor market share remains at ten per cent — half the twenty per cent 2030 target. The lab-to-fab gap, as SEMI calls it, persists because investment reaches pilot lines but not volume production.

The longer arc of regulatory accumulation

Europe's digital regulatory architecture is cumulative, not designed: each law assumed prior compliance as a baseline, adding layers without consolidating the stack. The General Data Protection Regulation set the template in 2016 — a law whose global influence through the Brussels Effect became the model for subsequent digital regulation. The Brookings Institution's February 2026 paper on AI sovereignty argues that the Brussels Effect gives the EU influence disproportionate to its technological capacity: seventeen per cent of global data centre capacity but zero foundation model control. Regulatory influence and industrial capacity have diverged rather than converged. ABN AMRO's analysis quantifies the tradeoff: each additional layer of sovereignty-focused regulation — from the Data Act's interoperability obligations to the AI Act's conformity assessments — adds compliance costs that reduce the capital available for deployment. BusinessEurope counts over one hundred tech-focused laws and two hundred and seventy regulators across the digital policy space. The Draghi report's diagnosis, delivered two weeks before the Siemens decision, named this accumulation as the structural problem. The Commission has not yet responded with a simplification measure that addresses the volume of rules rather than the mechanics of individual compliance.

Member states divided on the remedy

France and Germany agree on the diagnosis but diverge on the prescription. Paris frames the solution as strategic autonomy through industrial policy — more Chips Act-style instruments, more sovereign cloud procurement, more state aid for domestic AI champions under Article 107(3) TFEU. Berlin, since the Siemens decision, has shifted toward simplification: the German economy ministry now publicly supports the Commission's simplification agenda and has called for a regulatory "breathing space" for AI start-ups. The Netherlands and the Nordic states align with Berlin, arguing that capital will not return until the compliance burden falls. Southern and eastern member states, with less domestic tech industry at stake, prioritise the cohesion and defence spending channels in the 2026 budget. The simplification-versus-deregulation debate in the April plenary captured the fault line: the Commission's current proposals do not yet convince either camp that the distinction holds.

The September 2026 Chips Act evaluation and the resumption of the Digital Omnibus trilogue will be the first real test of whether the Commission can close the delivery gap from the regulatory side. If the evaluation confirms the lab-to-fab gap and the trilogue produces only a partial consolidation, the structural asymmetry will sharpen: defence sovereignty financed by public capital, tech sovereignty enforced by public rules. The next Commission cycle will need to determine whether those two tracks can converge — or whether Europe's sovereignty strategy remains a half-built bridge, funded on one end and regulated on the other.
