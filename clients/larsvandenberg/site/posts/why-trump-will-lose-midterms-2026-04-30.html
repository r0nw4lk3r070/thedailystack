Why Trump Will Lose the Midterms

30 April 2026 4 min read

Donald Trump's job approval sits at roughly 40%. Disapproval: 57%. On inflation, just 30% approve. On the economy, 37%. On healthcare, 29%. These are not the numbers of a president whose party holds ground in midterms. They are the numbers of a president whose coalition is coming apart — and the structural forces that typically punish an incumbent in November are not just present this year. They are amplified by conditions not seen since 1894.

This is not about one bad poll. It is about convergence: a historical midterm penalty, the first since 1894 non-consecutive second term, a fraying 2024 coalition, and a tariff regime that has driven consumer sentiment to an all-time low. For Europe, the stakes extend well beyond Washington. The EU's Anti-Coercion Instrument, activated in response to US tariffs, could counter these measures, with Germany and France leading the charge. A Democratic Congress might restrain Trump's tariffs on European goods, reopen transatlantic trade channels, and force a recalibration on NATO spending and tech sovereignty, potentially easing pressures on member states like the Netherlands.

The Numbers That Signal a Wave

The generic ballot tells the clearest story. Democrats lead by roughly 6 points nationally — an 8.5-point swing from the 2024 presidential result. The Cook Political Report estimates this swing would net Democrats about 21 House seats, enough to take the majority. Special elections in 2025 and 2026 have confirmed the direction: an average 15-point Democratic swing across contested races, from suburban Virginia to rural Iowa.

The Senate picture is narrower but real. Democrats lead in four races that would flip Republican-held seats: Maine by 7 points, North Carolina by 8, Alaska by 2, and Texas by 2 in Democratic internals. Netting four seats for a majority is plausible. The map still favours Republicans on paper. But the polling trajectory has moved sharply since January.

The 1894 Precedent

The 2026 midterms are the first during a non-consecutive second presidential term since Grover Cleveland's in 1894. That year, Cleveland's Democrats lost over 100 House seats and control of both chambers — still the largest House swing in American history. The parallel is not decorative. Cleveland, like Trump, returned to office after a gap term. Both faced economic disruption tied to tariff policy. Both saw their parties fractured between loyalists and pragmatists. Cleveland's party lost the midterms because voters who had returned him to office could not stomach the consequences of his economic agenda once it materialised. Trump is on the same trajectory.

The non-consecutive dynamic matters because the "return" coalition is inherently fragile. Voters who came back for Trump in 2024 did not sign up for tariffs on European imports or a trade war with China that raises consumer prices. The gap between what the 2024 coalition expected and what the 2025-26 agenda delivered is where the midterm penalty bites hardest.

A Coalition Coming Apart

Only 35% of Hispanic voters, 28% of young adults, and 27% of independents approve of Trump's performance. These three groups delivered his 2024 victory. Their disaffection now gives Democrats a 16-point generic ballot edge among the same demographics. Consumer sentiment hit a near-record low in April 2026. Just 27% of Americans are satisfied with the country's direction. Barely a third think the economy is on the right track.

Republican internal divisions compound the problem. GOP strategists disagree openly on whether Trump's tariffs will revive manufacturing or crash the economy. Some incumbents in tariff-exposed states — North Carolina, Maine, Alaska — are now distancing themselves from the White House trade agenda. That distance looks like the pre-2018 pattern, when incumbents tried to localise their races and lost anyway.

The question is not whether Trump will lose seats in November. The historical record makes that near-certain. The question is whether Democrats can convert structural advantage into governing power — and whether a Democratic Congress, if it materialises, will have the discipline to use that power on the tariff and trade questions that matter most to Europe.
