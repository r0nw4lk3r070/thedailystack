A Bilateral Hardening

2 May 2026 4 min read

On 25 April 2026, Emmanuel Macron and Kyriakos Mitsotakis renewed the Franco-Greek strategic defence partnership in Athens for another five years, with automatic renewal thereafter. The two leaders signed nine bilateral cooperation agreements covering defence, nuclear energy, education, and scientific research. Macron used the occasion to declare that Article 42.7 of the Treaty on European Union — the EU's mutual assistance clause — is "not just words" and is "stronger" than NATO's Article 5.

The renewal is the latest instance of France constructing European defence architecture through bilateral channels rather than through EU institutions alone. The original 2021 pact, signed in the wake of the AUKUS submarine crisis and heightened Eastern Mediterranean tensions, was already an outlier: a mutual defence commitment between two EU member states that goes further than anything the EU's own treaty framework provides in operational terms. Its extension, against the backdrop of the Iran war and an increasingly unreliable American security guarantee, exposes a structural fault line. European defence is being built in Paris, one bilateral pact at a time — not in Brussels.

The Pact Extended

The original Franco-Greek defence agreement was signed on 28 September 2021 by Macron and Mitsotakis. It included a mutual defence assistance clause: an armed attack against one would trigger the other's armed support. The pact was accompanied by a €3 billion procurement deal covering French Belharra-class frigates and corvettes for the Hellenic Navy. The 2026 renewal extends this framework for five more years and adds an automatic renewal mechanism — removing the need for future diplomatic renegotiation unless either party objects.

The nine new agreements signed in Athens go beyond the original defence scope. They cover nuclear energy cooperation, educational exchanges, and scientific research partnerships. Mitsotakis described the relationship as a "strong axis in the Mediterranean." Macron framed the mutual defence clause as unambiguous: "there is no room for interpretation or ambiguity." The inclusion of nuclear cooperation is deliberate. France is the EU's only nuclear-armed state with an independent deterrent. Extending that cooperation to Greece widens the security relationship beyond conventional naval procurement into strategic deterrence.

42.7 Versus 5

Macron's claim that Article 42.7 is "stronger" than Article 5 is the most consequential statement of the visit, and it deserves scrutiny. Article 42.7 commits EU member states to aid and assist any other member state that is the victim of armed aggression on its territory. It has been invoked once, by France after the 2015 Paris attacks. It has no standing operational plans, no integrated command structure, and no pre-assigned forces. NATO's Article 5, by contrast, operates under SACEUR with detailed operational plans and decades of integrated exercises.

Macron pointed to the activation of Article 42.7 in response to a drone attack on a British base in Cyprus on 28 February 2026, when several member states sent military aid to the island, as evidence that the clause works in practice. The comparison is instructive but incomplete. The Cyprus response was ad hoc and limited in scale. Article 5, for all its dependence on American strategic enablers, has institutional depth that 42.7 simply lacks. What Macron is arguing is not that 42.7 is operationally stronger today. He is arguing that it should be — and that bilateral pacts like the one with Greece are how France intends to close the gap.

Who Benefits, Who Watches

The Franco-Greek partnership serves both parties' strategic interests. For Greece, it provides a security guarantee from a major military power that is explicit about defending Greek sovereignty — a signal directed as much at Ankara as at any abstract threat. For France, it extends naval presence and political influence in the Eastern Mediterranean at a time when the US footprint in the region is contracting. Mitsotakis called France "Greece's true ally" and highlighted that the mutual assistance commitments are the centrepiece of the partnership.

Other member states are watching carefully. Italy, which competes with France for Mediterranean influence, has not sought a similar bilateral defence pact. The Netherlands and the Nordic states continue to channel defence investment through NATO and the EU's Permanent Structured Cooperation. Germany has focused on bilateral frameworks with Poland and the Baltics, but those are framed as NATO reinforcement, not as alternatives to it. The Franco-Greek model is not being replicated. It is a specific response to a specific set of pressures in the Eastern Mediterranean, and its generalisability remains untested.

If bilateral mutual defence pacts multiply across the EU, the result is a tiered security architecture where some member states hold bilateral guarantees and others rely on the thin institutional scaffolding of Article 42.7. If they do not multiply, the Franco-Greek pact becomes an exception that confirms the rule — and the rule is that European defence still runs through Washington, not through Brussels or Paris.
