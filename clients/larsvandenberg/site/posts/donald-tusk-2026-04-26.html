Tusk's Two-Track Hedging Strategy: French Nuclear Talks and an EU Defence Clause

In one week, Donald Tusk opened two parallel tracks to reduce Poland's dependence on American security guarantees. Neither track closes the door on Washington. Both assume the door might close on its own.

Gdańsk: Industrial Deliverables, Nuclear Vagueness

The 20 April intergovernmental summit with Emmanuel Macron produced concrete industrial outcomes. A military communications satellite jointly developed by Thales, Airbus and Polish firm Radmor. Expanded intelligence-sharing protocols. Joint military exercises. EDF formally pitching its EPR2 reactor for Poland's second nuclear power plant. These are real commitments with procurement timelines.

On the nuclear question, both leaders chose ambiguity. Macron said "there may be deployments" of French nuclear-armed Rafale warplanes to Poland and promised "concrete progress by summer." Tusk called the discussions "discreet" and said he wants clarity on "authority over these weapons" before committing. No agreement was signed. The gap between Macron's public signalling and Tusk's caution is itself telling: Paris wants the political effect of a nuclear umbrella; Warsaw wants the operational detail.

The venue carried a domestic signal. By hosting in Gdańsk — the birthplace of Solidarity — rather than Warsaw, and by excluding President Karol Nawrocki, Tusk made clear who runs Poland's foreign policy. Both Tusk and Macron disapproved of Nawrocki's recent visit to Viktor Orbán in Budapest. The Nancy Treaty's PM-to-President format provided formal cover. The snub was deliberate.

Nicosia: Activating Article 42.7

Four days later, at the informal European Council in Cyprus, Tusk championed a second track: making the EU's own mutual-assistance clause operational. Article 42.7 of the Treaty on European Union obliges member states to aid any member that is the victim of armed aggression. It has been invoked once — by France after the 2015 Paris attacks — and never in a military context.

Tusk strongly backed Cypriot President Nikos Christodoulides' proposal to prepare a blueprint for how Article 42.7 would function in practice. Leaders asked officials to draft one. The goal: a European defence backstop that complements NATO, not one that competes with it. This posture separates Tusk sharply from Orbán and Fico. He is pro-European and pro-alliance. His scepticism targets Washington's reliability, not NATO's purpose. He has said Polish-American relations remain "very important."

The Procurement Paradox

Poland already spends 4.7% of GDP on defence — the highest ratio in NATO — and Tusk has said he wants 5%. Warsaw is considering withdrawing from the Ottawa convention on anti-personnel landmines and the Dublin convention on cluster munitions. A plan to train every adult male, targeting a 500,000-strong force including reservists, is expected by year-end. Women may also participate. This is voluntary reserve training, not conscription.

Yet Poland's procurement list remains overwhelmingly American: F-35s, Apache helicopters, Patriot batteries, Abrams tanks. This creates unavoidable friction with Macron's insistence on a "European preference" for defence procurement. Tusk's strategic rhetoric points toward Paris and Brussels. His procurement reality still points across the Atlantic.

What Holds These Tracks Together

Tusk is not pivoting away from the United States. He is building redundancy. French nuclear consultations, an operational Article 42.7, and a 500,000-strong domestic reserve are three separate hedges against the same risk: that Washington, under pressure or by choice, does not honour Article 5 when it matters. The question is whether procurement habits will quietly undermine the strategic ambition — or whether the gap between what Poland buys and what Poland wants to build becomes the pressure that forces European defence industrial policy to deliver.
