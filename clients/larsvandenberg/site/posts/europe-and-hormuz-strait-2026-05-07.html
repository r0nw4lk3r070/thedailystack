Europe and the Strait of Hormuz

07 May 2026 4 min read

On 14 April 2026, the European Union and the United Kingdom announced EMAM-S — the European Maritime Awareness Mission in the Strait of Hormuz — a naval operation bringing together more than forty nations to protect commercial shipping through the world's most consequential energy chokepoint. France moved the Charles de Gaulle carrier group toward the region; Germany prepared to dispatch the minesweeper Fulda. The mission's mandate: escort vessels, clear mines, and restore freedom of navigation through a strait that had been closed since Iran's blockade following the US-Israel strikes.

The significance of EMAM-S lies less in its military scope than in its institutional architecture. The operation is not a CSDP mission. It was not launched under Article 42 TEU or authorised by a Council decision. It is an ad hoc intergovernmental coalition — led by France and the UK, coordinated with a parallel US-led force, and announced alongside rather than through the EU's formal defence structures. The strait crisis has forced Europe to act. The question the mission raises is whether the Union has the institutional capacity to act as a strategic actor, or whether it must always rely on willing coalitions operating outside the framework it spent decades building.

A coalition outside the framework

EMAM-S was announced at a Paris meeting on 14 April, days after a joint statement from the UK, France, Germany, Italy, the Netherlands, Japan, and Canada on 19 March signalled allied alignment on keeping Hormuz open. France and the UK serve as the operational leads. The Charles de Gaulle carrier group provides the mission's primary naval capability; Germany's contribution, the minesweeper Fulda, is expected to focus on mine clearance in the shipping lanes. The declared scope is defensive — escorting commercial vessels, ensuring safe passage, and clearing navigational hazards. Iran announced the reopening of the strait on 17 April, but the coalition has maintained its deployment, citing the need to verify that passage is genuinely secure before drawing down forces.

Two missions, one strait

The EMAM-S announcement coincided with the formation of a separate US-led Hormuz coalition. French officials have been careful to frame the two missions as complementary rather than competing — a position repeated by the French foreign ministry in early May. The distinction matters politically: European governments need to demonstrate autonomy of action without fracturing the broader Western alignment. Operationally, however, the relationship between the two coalitions remains unclear. No single authority coordinates both forces. Rules of engagement, command structures, and protocols for interaction with Iranian naval units have not been published. The result is a dual-track arrangement that holds as long as neither force encounters a situation requiring the other's support. A single escalation in the strait could overturn it.

The energy dependency underneath

Germany's participation in EMAM-S has been conditional from the outset. Berlin agreed to send the Fulda but attached reservations about the extent of US involvement in the European-led mission — a position reflecting a longstanding German discomfort with operations perceived as joining an American military campaign rather than a European security initiative. The Franco-German divergence on this point mirrors a structural tension: France treats the mission as proof that Europe can mount an independent naval operation; Germany treats it as a limited contribution to a broader allied effort.

Meanwhile, the energy calculus remains stark. Twelve to fourteen per cent of EU LNG imports come from Qatar via Hormuz. The Ifo Institute estimates that 6.2 per cent of EU crude oil and 8.7 per cent of LNG imports from non-EU states transit the strait. The crisis has already cost the EU an estimated €24 billion in additional fossil fuel import costs as European buyers competed with Asian markets for non-Qatari LNG. The post-2022 diversification away from Russian gas has not resolved Europe's dependency. It has shifted it from one chokepoint to another.

The structural lesson of EMAM-S is not that Europe cannot act, but that it cannot yet act through the institutions designed for the purpose. If the Union's CSDP framework is too slow or too constrained to launch a naval mission during an active energy crisis, the framework itself needs revision — not abandonment. The next test will come when the strait stabilises and the coalition draws down. Whether the EU uses that moment to build a standing maritime rapid-response capability, or reverts to waiting for the next crisis to force another ad hoc coalition, will reveal more about European strategic autonomy than any deployment order.
