Not at the Table

29 April 2026 5 min read

Not at the table

On 17 March 2026, Kaja Kallas said what no EU foreign policy chief had said in recent memory. She called on the United States and Israel to end a war they started without consulting Europe. "The main concern of the European countries is that we were not consulted," she told Reuters in Brussels. "Actually the opposite. There were many Europeans who were trying to convince the U.S. and Israel not to start this war." The statement was not a negotiation. It was a diagnosis. Washington no longer treats Brussels as a partner in decisions that reshape European energy markets, shipping routes, and security assumptions.

The Iran ceasefire of 8 April — which Kallas welcomed on behalf of the EU — did not undo the damage. The question is no longer whether the transatlantic relationship is strained, but whether the EU can build a foreign policy that functions without it.

The immediate rupture

The Iran war exposed the transatlantic fault line in real time. When Trump demanded European navies secure the Strait of Hormuz, multiple member states refused. Kallas instead proposed a diplomatic model drawn from the Black Sea grain deal — a UN-brokered arrangement that let Ukraine export through a war zone without military escort. She had discussed it with Secretary-General Guterres. The EU was pursuing a diplomatic track while its nominal ally demanded military participation. The ceasefire that came on 8 April resolved the immediate crisis. It did not resolve the asymmetry. Europe was not at the table when the war started, and it was not at the table when the war ended.

EU-Israel relations, meanwhile, hit their lowest point in decades. The trigger was not only the Iran war — it was a Russian shadow-fleet vessel carrying stolen Ukrainian grain, docked in Haifa. The EU signalled readiness to sanction Israel over it. More consequentially, an official review of the EU-Israel Association Agreement found "indications" that Israel had breached Article 2, the human rights clause. Spain, Ireland, and Slovenia pushed for suspension. At the 21 April Foreign Affairs Council in Luxembourg, the proposal failed for lack of consensus. The agreement stays. The signal to Israel is mixed. The signal to European publics is worse.

Strategic autonomy, imposed from outside

These crises sit on a longer trajectory. CIDOB frames 2026 as a year of "global readjustment" — technological and military rearmament intensifying alongside economic disparity and citizen discontent. The 2026 US National Defense Strategy made explicit what had been implicit: American commitment to European defence is now contingent. European allies are "expected to take the lead in the conventional defence of Europe," with America providing only "critical enablers." NATO formalised a 5% GDP defence spending benchmark. Strategic autonomy — once a French hobbyhorse — is now a functional requirement imposed by Washington's reprioritisation. HCSS analysis warns that spending pledges alone will not compensate for fragmented command structures, duplicated capabilities, and slow procurement cycles.

Divergent positions, constrained options

Member states do not share a diagnosis, let alone a prescription. Spain, Ireland, and Slovenia wanted the Association Agreement with Israel suspended. Others blocked it. On Hormuz, some capitals were open to a naval contribution; most were not. On defence spending, the 5% benchmark meets very different fiscal and political realities in Warsaw than in Rome.

India-EU relations offer a rare point of convergence. The February 2025 College of Commissioners visit to New Delhi — the first collective extraterritorial engagement of its kind — catalysed a "whole-of-partnership" framework covering trade, the Technology Council, green transition, and security dialogue. Bilateral trade surpassed $135 billion. The strategic depth is real. It is not decisive. India is not a substitute for the United States. It is a diversification.

The EU's problem in 2026 is not that it lacks partners. It is that it lacks a mechanism for turning partnership diversity into policy coherence. Kallas's Iran diplomacy showed the EU can act independently. The Association Agreement vote showed it cannot always agree. Strategic autonomy requires both.
