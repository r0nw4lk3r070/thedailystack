Europe Finds a New Security Partner in Canada

26 April 2026 4 min read

On 23 June 2025, the EU and Canada signed a Security and Defence Partnership (SDP) at the 20th EU-Canada Summit in Brussels. It is the EU's eighth such agreement and the first with a country from the Americas. The partnership itself is non-binding — a political framework, not a treaty. But the signal it sends is structural: Europe is building security relationships that do not depend on Washington.

What the partnership covers

The SDP groups twelve domains under a single political umbrella: defence policy, Ukraine support, military mobility, maritime security, cyber and hybrid threats, arms control, space security, emerging technology, economic security, and conflict prevention. It was co-signed by HRVP Kaja Kallas, Canadian Foreign Minister Anita Anand, and Defence Minister David McGuinty. The text states that the partnership "aims to underpin the mutually reinforcing EU-NATO strategic partnership" — complementary language, not competitive. In practice, it creates a bilateral channel for defence coordination that runs alongside NATO, not through it.

The non-binding character matters. An SDP carries no legal obligations and depends on political will for implementation. The EU has concluded similar partnerships with the United Kingdom, Norway, Japan, South Korea, and others. Canada is the first signatory from the Americas, and the first with both Arctic territory and a direct stake in European defence procurement.

The SAFE opportunity

The SDP's most tangible outcome is opening the door for Canadian participation in SAFE — the EU's €150 billion loan-backed joint procurement instrument under Readiness 2030. Canada concluded negotiations on a SAFE agreement in December 2025, but a separate technical deal must still be finalised before Canadian firms can bid on projects. The constraint is real: a "Buy European" clause requires at least 65% of weapons system value to be manufactured on European soil. Canadian defence companies would need to establish or invest in European production capacity to clear that threshold. The handshake is done. The industrial terms are not.

Strategic realignment

The SDP did not emerge in a vacuum. Both the EU and Canada face sustained US tariff pressure — 30% on EU goods, 35% on Canadian — and both are recalibrating their security posture in response to wavering American alliance commitments. Canada currently spends 1.37% of GDP on defence. At the 2025 NATO Summit in The Hague, Prime Minister Carney committed to 2% by the end of 2025 and 5% by 2035. The ambition outpaces the capability.

For the EU, Canada brings Arctic access, critical raw materials, and a partner for CSDP missions that shares both values and geography. A Canadian strategic nickel project under the EU Critical Raw Materials Act was flagged as a priority area for cooperation. For Canada, the partnership offers industrial opportunity through SAFE and a long-overdue diversification of security relationships beyond the United States.

Trade ties under pressure

The economic foundation is already in place. Bilateral trade grew 65% to €75.6 billion under CETA, but the deal remains unratified by ten EU member states — a persistent political gap that limits deeper integration. A Digital Trade Agreement was announced alongside the SDP to complement CETA. The security partnership adds a defence dimension to an economic relationship that is commercially deep but institutionally unfinished.

What comes next

An annual Security and Defence Dialogue between the EEAS and Global Affairs Canada will monitor implementation. The first meeting is expected later in 2026. The decisive question is whether the SAFE technical agreement will be finalised on terms that let Canadian firms compete meaningfully — and whether Canada can meet the European content requirements without simply subsidising European production. The SDP is a strong political signal. Whether it becomes a working defence partnership depends on negotiations that have not yet started.
