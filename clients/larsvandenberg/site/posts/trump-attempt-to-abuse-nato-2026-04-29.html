The Weaponisation of NATO: How Trump Is Using the Iran War to Break the Alliance

29 April 2026 4 min read

On 22 April 2026, POLITICO reported that the White House had compiled what officials described internally as a "naughty and nice" list of NATO allies, sorted by their willingness to support the US-led war against Iran. Countries that backed the campaign landed on one side. Several European allies — including those who refused combat operations, citing legal constraints on launching attacks from their territory (while some, like France, provided limited logistical support) — found themselves on the other, with concrete consequences under consideration. Days later, Reuters revealed that NATO itself was weighing whether to stop holding annual summits, in part to avoid encounters with a president who treats the alliance as a transactional instrument.

These are not separate crises. They are three faces of the same attempt to repurpose NATO from a mutual defence pact into a loyalty-testing mechanism — and the alliance is already bending under the pressure.

The Punishment Mechanism

The "naughty list" is not rhetorical. According to POLITICO, confirmed by the Wall Street Journal, the White House is actively considering how to penalise allies who declined to join the Iran campaign. The measures under discussion include relocating US troops away from uncooperative countries, pulling American officers from NATO command positions, and potentially closing a US base in Europe. The message is straightforward: support the war, or lose the security architecture that has underwritten European defence for seven decades.

The irony, as former Deputy Assistant Secretary of Defense Jim Townsend noted, is that there is no punishment the US can inflict on an ally that does not also hurt American interests. Moving troops out of European allies' territory degrades the logistical chains that support US operations beyond Europe. Withholding consensus at NATO headquarters paralyses the alliance's ability to act on any issue, including those the US prioritises.

The Legal Fiction and the Political Reality

Trump has repeatedly this year threatened to withdraw the United States from NATO. He cannot do it alone. The 2023 National Defence Authorization Act prohibits unilateral withdrawal from NATO without a two-thirds Senate vote or an act of Congress. The legal barrier is real.

But the legal barrier is not the political barrier. As former NATO ambassador Ivo Daalder has argued, the damage is not in formal withdrawal — it is in the erosion of Article 5 credibility. When the US president publicly questions whether the alliance is worth belonging to, every ally recalculates. The commitment that once seemed ironclad becomes conditional. Trust, once spent, does not return with the next administration. Pew polling already shows Republican favourability toward NATO membership declining, suggesting the corrosion could outlast Trump's presidency.

An Alliance Restructuring Around One Man

The most striking signal is not what Trump is doing to NATO — it is what NATO is doing to itself. Reuters reported that the alliance is considering ending annual summits, possibly shifting to biennial meetings. This is an institutional adaptation to presidential hostility. NATO is reshaping its own architecture to avoid a confrontation with the American president.

Annual summits are where leaders make strategic decisions, set spending targets, and signal unity. The Hague summit in 2025 produced the 5 percent GDP defence spending pledge — a commitment Trump himself pushed for. Now, the alliance is discussing whether it is better off not meeting at all than meeting with Trump in the room.

Secretary General Mark Rutte has called for a shift from "unhealthy co-dependence to a healthy partnership," and a NATO Transition Planning Group is under discussion to coordinate the US capability drawdown in Europe. The language is diplomatic. The substance is a managed separation.

The Ankara summit in July 2026 will be the test. Turkey Today has described it as potentially the most consequential in NATO history. The Atlantic Council has proposed constructive off-ramps — Arctic Sentry deployments to address the Greenland dispute, NATO-led Hormuz maritime operations to share the burden on Iran. But these proposals require a US president willing to accept partial solutions rather than total compliance. Nothing in Trump's track record suggests he will.

What happens when an alliance built on consensus faces a member who treats consensus as a weapon? The Ankara summit may answer that question — or it may demonstrate that the answer has already been settled.
