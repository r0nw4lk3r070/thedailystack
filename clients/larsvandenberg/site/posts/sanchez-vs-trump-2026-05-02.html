Treaty Architecture vs Coercion: Why Spain Survived Trump's Threats

2 May 2026 4 min read

On 2 March 2026, Spanish Prime Minister Pedro Sánchez told the United States it could not use the bases at Rota and Morón for strikes on Iran. Within 48 hours, Donald Trump threatened to cut off all trade with Spain. A leaked Pentagon email in April then floated suspending Spain from NATO altogether. None of it worked. Spain closed its airspace to aircraft involved in Operation Epic Fury, banned transit of US arms shipments to Israel via its territory, and became the only NATO member to oppose raising the alliance's spending target from 2% to 5% of GDP. Far from retreating, Sánchez doubled down.

The confrontation reveals a structural asymmetry that Washington has yet to reckon with. Trade policy is an exclusive Union competence — the United States cannot impose tariffs on Spain alone without doing so against all 27 member states. NATO's founding treaty contains no mechanism for expulsion. And the bases the Pentagon treats as leverage are sovereign Spanish installations that require Madrid's authorisation for every activity. The institutional architecture held. What looks like a bilateral dispute is in fact a test of how much coercion an alliance structure can absorb before the institutions themselves push back.

Spain's refusal triggered threats that could not move Madrid

On 2 March 2026, the Spanish government formally denied the United States permission to use Rota and Morón for strikes on Iran as part of Operation Epic Fury. US tankers and aircraft relocated to Germany and France within days. Spain then went further: it closed its entire airspace to flights involved in the operation and banned transit of US arms shipments to Israel via Spanish territory. The White House claimed Madrid had agreed to cooperate. Madrid denied it. When Trump threatened to "cut off" trade with Spain on 4 March, the Spanish central bank assessed direct tariff exposure at roughly 3% of GDP — but the IBEX index rallied. Markets understood what the White House did not: the Union, not Spain, holds trade competence. By April, a leaked Pentagon email proposed suspending Spain from NATO and reassessing the US position on the Falkland Islands. NATO's secretary-general noted publicly that the Washington Treaty contains no provision for expelling a member state.

Treaty architecture insulated Spain from every threat

The legal structure that protects Spain was not designed for this moment, but it functions regardless. Under Article 207(1) of the Treaty on the Functioning of the European Union, trade policy is an exclusive Union competence — the United States cannot impose tariffs on Spain without imposing them on all 27 member states, a step that would rupture the broader EU-US trade framework that von der Leyen negotiated down to a 15% tariff deal in July 2025. At NATO, Article 12 of the Washington Treaty allows a member to withdraw with one year's notice, but there is no mechanism for expulsion. The most the alliance can do is downgrade intelligence sharing or restrict participation in joint planning — measures that hurt the alliance as much as the target. The bases themselves are governed by the 1988 bilateral Defence Cooperation Agreement between Spain and the United States. Both Rota and Morón remain sovereign Spanish installations under Spanish command. Madrid's authorisation is required for all activity. The Pentagon's own retaliation options concede this.

European solidarity held where the cost was rhetorical

Sánchez's defiance has drawn support, but not uniformly. France and Germany backed Spain publicly, both governments framing the base refusal as a sovereign right. The Hague summit in June gave Spain flexibility on capability targets rather than imposing the 5% GDP spending benchmark. Yet other member states — particularly those hosting US bases in eastern Europe — have stayed quiet, wary of inviting similar pressure. ECFR polling shows only 10% of Spaniards view the United States as an ally, a figure that predates the current crisis. That gives Sánchez domestic room to manoeuvre. But the silence from Warsaw and Bucharest reveals the limit of European solidarity: it holds when the cost is rhetorical, frays when it might jeopardise bilateral security arrangements with Washington.

The Spain-US confrontation has already altered the calculus for other European governments watching from the margin. If treaty architecture alone can absorb a direct US threat without material consequence for the resisting state, the precedent weakens the coercive tool for future disputes — not just over base access, but over defence spending, technology transfer, and China policy. Sánchez's refusal has shown that a member state can say no to Washington and survive. Whether that precedent holds when a less domestically secure government faces a White House willing to test the alliance's seams remains the structural risk.
