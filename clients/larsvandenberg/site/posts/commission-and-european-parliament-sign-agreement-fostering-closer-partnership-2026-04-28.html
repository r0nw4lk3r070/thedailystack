Commission and Parliament Sign Revised Framework Agreement

28 April 2026 4 min read

On 28 April 2026, Ursula von der Leyen and Roberta Metsola signed the revised Framework Agreement between the European Commission and the European Parliament. It is the first update to the inter-institutional rulebook since 2010. The Parliament had already approved it on 11 March — 446 in favour, 100 against, 72 abstentions.

Fifteen years is a long gap. The EU that produced the 2010 text had just absorbed the Lisbon Treaty's promise of parliamentary empowerment. The EU that signed this one has lived through sovereign debt crises, pandemic emergency procurement, and a war that forced energy policy into overdrive. Parliament has spent those years demanding — and often seizing — more say. The revised agreement tries to codify what has already shifted in practice. Whether it goes further than that is the real question.

What changed

The most substantive addition is the so-called negative right of initiative. Parliament can now formally call on the Commission to revise or withdraw existing legislation. This is not the full right of initiative that MEPs have long demanded — the Commission keeps its monopoly on proposing new laws. But it gives Parliament a procedural lever to push for repeal or amendment that did not exist under the 2010 text.

The emergency clause has been tightened. When the Commission invokes an emergency procedure to accelerate or bypass the ordinary legislative track, it must provide full justification to Parliament immediately. During the pandemic, emergency procurement and vaccine contracts were negotiated with minimal parliamentary oversight. This provision does not remove the Commission's emergency powers. It forces transparency.

On international agreements, the Commission must now inform Parliament early when it intends to apply a treaty provisionally, and must allow sufficient time for the consent procedure. Trade deals and association agreements have repeatedly arrived at Parliament's door as faits accomplis. The new text narrows that gap.

There is also a reciprocal accountability mechanism: the Commission commits to fostering Commissioner presence in plenary and committee when requested, while Parliament commits to regularly assessing its own members' plenary attendance. It is an unusual mutual obligation — the Commission gains a procedural argument for calling out absenteeism; Parliament gains a stronger claim on Commissioner availability.

The institutional arc

The 2010 Framework Agreement was a product of the post-Lisbon settlement. The Lisbon Treaty had given Parliament co-legislative status with the Council on most policy areas. The agreement was designed to manage that new reality — how the Commission informs Parliament, when it consults, what it shares.

Fifteen years on, Parliament has outgrown those rules in practice. The Conference of Presidents has demanded earlier and fuller Commission briefings. The European Court of Auditors and the ECB have faced scrutiny that goes well beyond what the 2010 text envisaged. The revised agreement tries to catch up with practice. Whether practice will simply run ahead again is another matter.

The politics behind the text

Both von der Leyen and Metsola are EPP leaders. That alliance matters. The Framework Agreement was negotiated and approved without the institutional warfare that has marked previous Commission–Parliament standoffs. But the political backdrop is not harmonious. Parliament has been pushing back on the Commission's €865 billion budget proposal. Metsola has warned that "Europe cannot face a new era with an old budget." The same Parliament that signed this agreement is the one threatening to reject the Commission's spending plans.

The 100 votes against and 72 abstentions in March reflect scepticism on two fronts. Greens and the Left argued the agreement does not go far enough on transparency and democratic control. Parts of the right questioned whether Parliament should formalise closer ties with a Commission they consider overreaching. Both criticisms miss the point. The Framework Agreement is not a treaty change. It does not alter the EU's constitutional architecture. But it shifts the procedural terrain — and in Brussels, procedure is power.

A Parliament that can demand justification for emergency measures, call for the withdrawal of existing laws, and insist on early notification of international deals has more leverage than one that cannot. Whether it uses that leverage depends on political will, not institutional text.
