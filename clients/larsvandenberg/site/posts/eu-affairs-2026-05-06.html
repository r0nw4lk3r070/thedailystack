EDIP and SEAP: the community method extends into defence

6 May 2026 3 min read

On 28 January 2026, German Finance Minister Lars Klingbeil and French counterpart Bruno Le Maire launched the E6 Initiative — a coordination format for the finance ministers of Germany, France, Italy, the Netherlands, Poland, and Spain. Its four-point mandate covers capital markets union, euro reinforcement, defence investment coordination, and critical raw materials access. Each of these maps onto competences where the European Commission holds legislative initiative or exclusive authority. Meanwhile, the European Defence Investment Programme — EDIP, Regulation 2025/2643 — is entering implementation, establishing the first permanent EU-funded defence procurement framework and its cooperative armament mechanism, the Structure for European Armament Programme (SEAP). Two institutional tracks. One unresolved question: who decides?

The tension is structural. EDIP operates through the community method — Commission proposal, co-decision, EU-budget funding. SEAP requires a minimum of three participating entities from three member states, but the Commission positions it as the preferred channel for EU-funded procurement. The E6, by contrast, is an intergovernmental format with no treaty basis, no Commission seat at the table, and no obligation to report to the Council. Defence capacity is being built. The institutional architecture governing it remains contested.

EDIP and SEAP: the community method extends into defence

EDIP funds three categories of defence activity: common procurement, industrial reinforcement, and security of supply. SEAP is the mechanism through which participating states coordinate the procurement, development, and lifecycle management of defence systems. It is the Commission's preferred instrument — a structured, EU-budget-backed process that extends the community method into a policy domain long governed by national sovereignty and intergovernmental agreement.

Ukraine's inclusion in EDIP for SEAP-based procurement adds geopolitical weight to what might otherwise read as a procedural innovation. The regulation does not merely create a procurement tool. It embeds a third country in an EU defence mechanism during wartime. The institutional design choices inside SEAP — how procurement calls are structured, how competitive requirements are applied, how sovereignty safeguards operate — carry consequences far beyond procurement efficiency.

The E6: intergovernmental coordination outside the framework

The E6 Initiative emerged from a different institutional logic. Klingbeil's correspondence to his counterparts framed the group as a response to structural shifts and an increasingly volatile geopolitical environment. The four-point strategy — capital markets union, euro reinforcement, defence investment coordination, critical raw materials — maps directly onto areas where the Commission holds legislative initiative or exclusive competence. The E6 does not propose new legislation. It coordinates positions among the six largest economies ahead of EU decision-making.

Klingbeil has advocated differentiated integration — groups of willing member states advancing faster in selected policy areas. E6 officials frame the format as providing momentum that others can join. The framing is inclusive. The structure is not. Six finance ministers coordinating outside formal EU structures, on competences that belong to the Commission, is not a complement to the community method. It is a parallel track that sets the agenda before smaller states and EU institutions have a seat at the table.

Article 42(7): where the fault line surfaces

The same tension runs through the debate over Article 42(7) TEU, the mutual assistance clause. Friedrich Merz has called it "not a substitute for NATO." Ursula Von der Leyen has said "the time has come to bring Europe's mutual defence clause to life." The divergence is not about the clause itself. It is about who activates it, under whose authority, and through which institutional channel.

The SIEPS analysis of European defence governance identifies a persistent "capability-expectations gap" — a term drawn from Christopher Hill's 1993 framework. Geopolitical pressure converges: member states agree on the need for defence capacity. Institutional and political fragmentation diverges: sovereignty concerns, competing institutional claims, and differentiated integration models produce parallel structures that may reinforce or undermine each other depending on political will that has not yet been tested.

EDIP is law. SEAP is operational. The E6 is convening. The institutional logic that governs how this capacity is directed — community method or intergovernmental coordination — will shape European integration for the next decade. Which track sets the default is the question the next European Council will have to answer.
