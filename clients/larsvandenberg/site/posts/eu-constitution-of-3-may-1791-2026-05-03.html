Europe's First Constitution Was Polish — And Its Structural Defect Still Shapes the EU

3 May 2026 4 min read

On 3 May 1791, the Polish-Lithuanian Commonwealth's Great Sejm adopted the Government Act — a written constitution that introduced tripartite separation of powers, abolished the liberum veto, established an independent judiciary, and extended political rights to the bourgeoisie. It was Europe's first modern written constitution, predating the French by months. Within 14 months, Russian military intervention forced its suspension. Within four years, the Commonwealth ceased to exist. The EU now recognises the 3 May Constitution as a European Heritage Label site, linking it to the Union's own constitutional values. The connection between the 1791 document and the EU's present rule-of-law architecture is not symbolic. It is structural.

Poland produced the continent's first written constitution. Poland also became the first EU member state against which Article 7 TEU was activated — in December 2017, for systematic executive interference in the judiciary. The same principle the 1791 constitution sought to insulate — judicial independence from executive power — was the one the European Commission found under sustained assault. The European Heritage Label frames the 1791 constitution as shared inheritance. The Article 7 procedure frames contemporary Poland as a threat to that inheritance. Both framings describe the same institutional reality: constitutional texts do not enforce themselves.

The 1791 constitution corrected a paralysis the EU later reintroduced

The Government Act's central institutional reform was the abolition of the liberum veto — the right of any single deputy to nullify a Sejm decision. The veto had paralysed the Commonwealth for over a century, enabling foreign powers to buy a single deputy and block any reform. The 1791 constitution replaced unanimity with majority voting, introduced a hereditary monarchy to prevent foreign electoral interference, and established three separated branches: a bicameral Sejm and Senate as legislature, the King and his ministers as executive, and independent courts as judiciary. Reformers advanced the vote from 5 May to 3 May because Russian-backed deputies were expected to block it. The external pressure that shaped the adoption was the same force that destroyed it after the Russo-Polish War of 1792.

Article 7 reproduced the defect the 1791 constitution eliminated

The liberum veto has a structural analogue in the EU's rule-of-law enforcement architecture. Article 7(2) TEU requires unanimity in the European Council to impose sanctions on a member state — a provision Hungary and Poland used to shield each other from 2017 onwards. The mechanism designed to protect the Union's constitutional values contains the same defect the 1791 constitution corrected: a single veto can block collective action against a systemic threat. Article 7's preventive arm was added at the Nice European Council in 2003, partly in response to concerns about the 2004 enlargement. The EU built its rule-of-law enforcement mechanism precisely as it admitted the countries with the deepest constitutional traditions but the weakest institutional resilience. The design assumed goodwill. It did not account for a member state capturing its own judiciary.

Creation and resilience are not the same measure

Poland's democratic lineage extends well beyond 1791. The 1505 Nihil Novi act — "nothing new without the common consent" — established a proto-parliamentary principle that predates most Western European constitutional orders. Yet the 1791 constitution survived 14 months. The 1921 March Constitution survived 18 years before the Sanacja regime hollowed it out. The 1997 Constitution, adopted after the fall of communism, survived intact only until 2015, when the PiS government began its systematic overhaul of the Constitutional Tribunal, the Supreme Court, and the National Council of the Judiciary. Constitutional creation in Poland has repeatedly outpaced constitutional resilience. The 1791 constitution's suppression by Russia, Prussia, and Austria foreshadows a recurring condition in Central and Eastern Europe, where constitutional orders have been overwritten by external or domestic anti-constitutional forces with a regularity that Western European institutional designers have never fully absorbed.

The EU's rule-of-law framework still assumes that constitutional resilience follows automatically from constitutional creation — that admitting a state with a written constitution is sufficient. The 3 May Constitution survived 14 months. The lesson is not that the document was insufficient. The lesson is that the institutions designed to enforce its principles were weaker than the forces arrayed against them. The EU's Article 7 mechanism, paralysed by its own unanimity requirement, replicates the defect. Constitutional heritage without enforcement architecture is ceremony.
