The rally in numbers

2 May 2026 3 min read

The rally in numbers

The Spring 2025 Eurobarometer records 74% of EU citizens saying their country benefits from EU membership — the highest figure since the question was first asked in 1983. In the same survey window, favourable views of the United States across the EU collapsed from 47% to 29%, an 18-point drop in under six months. Denmark fell from 47% to 13%. Poland dropped 19 points. France fell 26.

The numbers tell a story of external shock, not organic enthusiasm. 62% of citizens want the EU to play a more prominent role globally. Yet only 44% expect it will. The gap between aspiration and confidence is 18 points, and it matters. People want more from the Union. Most do not believe the Union will deliver.

The institutional response

The sentiment surge coincides with a defence spending acceleration that predates it but has been amplified by it. EU defence expenditure rose 60% between 2020 and 2025. In March 2025, Commission President von der Leyen launched "Readiness 2030" — the renamed successor to ReArm Europe — mobilising €800 billion in defence financing. The European Parliament majority backed the plan, while calling for long-term strategic autonomy rather than rearmament alone.

The Foreign Affairs argument, advanced by figures like González Laya, holds that Europe possesses the wealth and talent to sustain itself independently. The institutional machinery is beginning to align with that proposition. But alignment is not commitment. The Eurobarometer data shows 51% of citizens still believe the EU is heading in the wrong direction. A rally-around-the-flag moment can produce legislative momentum. It can also produce overreach followed by backlash.

Defence spending can be raised and lowered in budget cycles. Treaty reform, strategic procurement frameworks, and institutional consolidation take years and require durable political consent. The current sentiment provides a window. It does not guarantee it stays open.

The fault lines

The surge is not uniformly distributed. The US favourability collapse is concentrated in traditionally Atlanticist states. ECFR polling across 14 European states found the most common classification of the US shifted from "ally" to "necessary partner" — transactionality, not trust. Even Poland and Denmark now place the US in that diminished frame. Meanwhile, Romania saw US favourability rise 2 points. Bulgaria rose 1 point. Greece and Cyprus remained flat. These states also record higher pro-Russian attitudes — Cyprus at 49%, Bulgaria at 44%, Greece at 38% positive on Russia. The pattern is consistent: states with weaker institutional trust in the EU, or stronger historical ties to non-EU powers, are not participating in the rally.

China did not benefit either. EU27 favourability toward China held steady at 29% — equal to the new US figure. The disillusionment with Washington is not producing a pivot to Beijing. It is producing a turn inward, toward European institutions, or toward no one at all.

Hungary and Slovakia remain sceptical of deeper integration, and their governments reflect that. ECFR data shows supporters of Europe's far-right parties — the AfD in Germany, Fratelli d'Italia in Italy, PiS and Konfederacja in Poland — are notably more optimistic about Trump's return than the general public. A transatlantic current is visible: far-right voters in Europe aligning with MAGA-aligned politics in the United States, forming a political bloc that the pro-European surge leaves behind or actively pushes against.

The 74% figure describes a majority, but the minority it excludes holds governing power in several member states. The Hungarian and Slovak positions are not marginal dissent. They are structural vetoes within the Council. Any institutional deepening that requires unanimity — treaty reform, common defence commitments, foreign policy coordination — runs through Budapest and Bratislava. The sentiment data does not resolve that constraint. It intensifies it.

The real test is not the Eurobarometer figure. It is whether the Commission and the Council can convert crisis-driven consensus into treaty-grade commitment while the holdouts still hold veto power. The window is open. The architecture for walking through it is not yet built.
