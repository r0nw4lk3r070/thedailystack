Galileo, Copernicus, and the Gap Between Sovereignty Rhetoric and Infrastructure Reality

10 May 2026 5 min read

On 7 April 2026, the European Commission published proposal COM(2026)152 to recast EUSPA as the European Union Space Services Agency, granting it a standalone legal basis decoupled from the 2021–2027 Multiannual Financial Framework. The recast expands the agency's mandate to cover security accreditation — including Galileo's Public Regulated Service — operational service delivery, market uptake, and crisis continuity. It arrives at a moment when Europe's sovereign navigation constellation still cannot field the encrypted military-grade signal that was the programme's founding rationale. Galileo was conceived in 1999. Its open service reached initial operational capability in December 2016. The restricted signal remains unaccredited.

The structural stakes run deeper than any single agency reform. Galileo and Copernicus together have consumed €18 billion, yet governance remains split across the Commission, ESA, EUSPA, and 27 member states with divergent industrial interests. Copernicus, the programme with the larger downstream multiplier, receives weaker regulatory push and fragmented uptake support than Galileo, which commands political attention precisely because positioning looks like a military question. The Commission's proposal addresses one crack. Whether it reaches the load-bearing walls is a different matter.

The sovereignty gap, quantified

Galileo's Public Regulated Service — the encrypted, jam-resistant signal designed for government and military use — remains unaccredited in 2026, 18 years after the original 2008 target date. The Commission told the European Parliament's ITRE committee it expects PRS accreditation "soon," the same formulation it has used for years. Aviation Week now reports a 2026 operational date, slipping from end-2025. Meanwhile, a European Defence Fund call for integrating PRS receivers into weapon systems and command centres confirms that military adoption sits at the integration stage, not deployment. The launch dependency tells a parallel story. Between April and September 2024, ESA contracted SpaceX to launch four Galileo satellites on Falcon 9 — the sovereign constellation riding an American vehicle because Ariane 5 had retired, Ariane 6 had not yet flown, and Soyuz access was lost after Russia's invasion of Ukraine in 2022. Ariane 6 carried Galileo L14 on 17 December 2025. EUSPA signed a second-generation launch contract with Arianespace on 27 January 2026. The gap narrows. It has not closed.

Cost overruns are the norm, not the exception

Galileo's original 1999 cost estimate ranged from €2.2 to 2.9 billion, with full operational capability expected by 2008. By the time initial services launched in December 2016, cumulative spending had reached roughly €10 billion — over triple the original estimate — and full capability remains outstanding. The pattern is not exceptional. Ariane, born from a decision at the end of 1973 to end European dependence on US launchers, took a decade to fly and another to compete commercially before becoming the world's leading commercial launch provider through the 2000s. Airbus, formed in 1970 to break American dominance in commercial aircraft, sustained prolonged losses before the A320 family made it credible. European strategic autonomy projects consistently overshoot by two to three times. Several eventually deliver. Copernicus adds a different dimension. The European Court of Auditors, in Special Report 21/07, found that Commission actions to support Copernicus uptake were fragmented and underfunded, and that the Commission did not sufficiently promote Earth Observation in its regulations. The combined GNSS and Earth Observation downstream sector, valued at €200 billion in 2021 and projected toward €500 billion, receives less regulatory push than a navigation programme whose signature military signal remains unaccredited.

Three institutional logics, one sovereignty promise

The recast proposal responds to a structural problem: EUSPA was created inside Regulation (EU) 2021/696, its mandate tied to a budget cycle, even though it was never meant to expire with that cycle. The recast would give it a standalone legal basis and expand its mandate across security accreditation, service delivery, and crisis continuity. But the deeper friction lies between three institutional logics. ESA, an intergovernmental body with 23 members including non-EU states Norway and Switzerland, applies a geographic return principle that ties industrial contracts to national contribution levels. EUSPA, as an EU agency, operates under EU procurement rules. The Commission sets programme direction but relies on both. Analyses by the CEPS Task Force on European Space Leadership and ESPI's governance studies identify split authority — between the Commission, ESA, and EUSPA, compounded by 27 member states with divergent industrial interests — as a central obstacle to delivering on sovereignty commitments. The recast is necessary. Whether it is sufficient depends on whether member states cede enough coordination authority to make the new agency functional, or whether distributed responsibility and concentrated delay persist.

The EUSPA recast will be debated in the European Parliament and Council through late 2026. The test is not whether the agency receives a new name and legal basis — that is procedural. The test is whether member states accept expanded EU-level authority over security accreditation and procurement coordination, areas where national industrial interests have long held veto power. If the recast passes with its mandate intact, Europe gains a single institutional home for sovereign space services. If member states carve out exemptions to protect geographic return arrangements, the reform reproduces the fragmentation it was designed to resolve. Ariane and Airbus eventually delivered because political will outlasted institutional delay. Galileo and Copernicus now face the same structural question — with the difference that the sovereignty they promise is measurable in signals yet to be accredited and data lacking regulatory mandate for mandatory use.
