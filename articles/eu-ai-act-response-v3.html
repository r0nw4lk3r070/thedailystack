The EU AI Act Is Not Overregulation — It Is Europe's Last Sovereignty Shield

Ron Spoelstra · April 2026 · 7 min read

When someone calls the EU AI Act an "absolute abomination," the instinct is to argue about compliance costs. That is exactly the wrong debate. The real question is not whether the Act is burdensome — it is whether Europe has any structural lever left against unchecked US tech dominance. The answer, right now, is barely.

The Wrong Debate

A recent exchange on X between @actsmaniac, who called the EU AI Act an "absolute abomination," and @txgermanbre, who pushed back with deep expertise on the Act's design, captured a fault line that runs far deeper than social media spats. The repeal camp frames the Act purely as regulatory burden — compliance costs, innovation throttling, competitive disadvantage versus the US and China. The sovereignty camp understands it as a structural power instrument: it gives Europe the ability to set terms for how AI operates in its market, creates enforcement leverage over foreign providers, and establishes a global regulatory template through the Brussels Effect.

The real fault line is not "regulation versus innovation." It is European self-determination versus vassalisation. The repeal argument implicitly assumes that US tech firms should set the terms of AI deployment in Europe. The defense argument recognises the Act as one of the few tools Europe has to assert its own digital sovereignty. Once you see this framing, you cannot unsee it — and you start noticing who benefits from the "overregulation" narrative.

The Vance Doctrine: Deregulation as Geopolitics

At the Paris AI Action Summit on 11 February 2025, US Vice President JD Vance delivered what amounted to a declaration of regulatory war. "We believe that excessive regulation of the AI sector could kill a transformative industry just as it's taking off," he said. He warned that "some foreign governments are considering tightening the screws on U.S. tech companies," and pledged that "American AI will not be co-opted into a tool for authoritarian censorship." His four-point plan was unambiguous: American AI as the global gold standard, deregulation as the default, ideological freedom as the framing, and pro-worker growth as the promise.

Vance was not speaking in a vacuum. Eleven days later, on 22 February 2025, President Trump signed a memorandum directing his administration to consider tariffs against any country that taxes, fines, or regulates American tech companies. The White House specifically named the Digital Markets Act and the Digital Services Act as targets for scrutiny. This was not a suggestion. It was a trade policy weapon aimed at the entire European regulatory stack.

The US and UK were the only major nations to refuse signing the Paris AI declaration, endorsed by over 60 countries including China. This was not about the declaration's content. It was about refusing to legitimise any multilateral AI governance framework that the US does not control.

European Commission President Ursula von der Leyen responded at the same summit with a concession that revealed the pressure: "AI needs the confidence of the people and has to be safe" — followed immediately by "At the same time, I know that we have to make it easier and we have to cut red tape and we will." The retreat was audible.

The Digital Omnibus: Sovereignty by a Thousand Cuts

The pressure did not stop at rhetoric. In 2025, the European Commission proposed the Digital Omnibus — a package of amendments to the AI Act that delays high-risk AI compliance deadlines by up to 16 months, pushing obligations for organisations deploying AI in hiring, loan assessment, and other high-risk domains from August 2026 to December 2027. The Council agreed on its position in March 2026.

The Commission framed this as "simplification." Critics, including researchers writing in The Conversation, called it what it is: a policy shift that prioritises Big Tech over fairness. The delay does not affect the risk-classification framework itself, but it sends a clear signal — Europe's resolve is negotiable under US pressure. Each delay, each simplification, each rollback cuts a little deeper into the Act's function as a sovereignty instrument. Not a dramatic repeal, but a quiet erosion.

WIRED reported that Europe is "bending the knee" to US tech policy, citing cancelled measures and fewer restrictions on Big Tech under Trump administration pressure. Le Monde documented how Washington and American tech giants coordinated an offensive against the law as it took effect in August 2025. The pattern is consistent: external pressure, internal concession, framed as pragmatism.

The Brussels Effect — And Why America Wants to Kill It

The concept of the Brussels Effect, developed by Anu Bradford, explains how EU regulation shapes global standards. Once the EU sets rules for its massive internal market, companies worldwide adapt to those rules rather than maintaining separate compliance regimes. The GDPR proved this: it did not just protect European data — it forced every major tech company on the planet to build privacy controls, hire data protection officers, and offer opt-out mechanisms. The EU set the global standard without firing a shot.

The AI Act has the same structural potential. It creates a risk-based classification system that applies to any AI system operating in the EU market, regardless of where it was developed. High-risk systems — those used in hiring, credit scoring, law enforcement, critical infrastructure — face mandatory transparency, documentation, and human oversight requirements. General-purpose AI models face disclosure obligations. These are not radical demands. They are the minimum conditions for democratic accountability over technology that affects people's lives.

The US tech lobby understands this better than most European policymakers. The Brussels Effect means that if the EU holds the line on AI regulation, American companies will have to comply with European rules globally — not because they want to, but because maintaining two separate compliance regimes is more expensive than building one system to the highest standard. That is why the fight is so intense. The stakes are not European. They are global.

What Happens If Europe Keeps Yielding

The GDPR precedent is instructive. Despite years of US pressure, Europe held the line on data privacy regulation. The result was not economic collapse — it was a global standard that gave Europe normative power and forced the world's largest tech companies to comply with European rules. The AI Act could do the same for artificial intelligence. But only if Europe stops conceding.

The concrete sovereignty risks of continued erosion are threefold. First, without enforceable AI rules, US hyperscalers set the terms of AI deployment in Europe through market dominance alone — no European input, no European oversight. Second, the Brussels Effect collapses: if Europe signals it will not enforce its own rules, other jurisdictions have no incentive to adopt similar frameworks, and the global regulatory vacuum is filled by US corporate interests. Third, the tariff weapon works both ways: if Europe demonstrates that regulatory pressure makes it fold, the pressure will not stop at AI. It will extend to data, platforms, cloud infrastructure, and every other domain where US tech dominance intersects with European sovereignty.

Belgium's own Imec, the Leuven-based nanoelectronics and AI research centre, exemplifies the stakes. Imec develops AI hardware and edge computing solutions that depend on a regulatory environment where European research institutions can compete on fair terms with US hyperscalers. Weakening the AI Act does not help Imec innovate — it removes the structural conditions that make European AI research viable in a market dominated by American infrastructure. The same logic applies to Ghent-based ML6, which builds custom AI solutions for European industry and needs clear, enforceable rules to operate without being squeezed by unregulated American competitors.

The Act Is Not the Problem — The Absence of an Alternative Is

The EU AI Act is not perfect. It is complex, its risk-classification system has real compliance costs, and its Brussels Effect may be weaker than the GDPR's. But the alternative is not a leaner, better regulation. The alternative is no European counterweight at all. Calls to repeal the Act are not about unleashing European innovation — they are about eliminating Europe's ability to set its own digital terms. The @actsmaniac framing treats regulation as a cost with no benefit. The @txgermanbre framing recognises it as the price of sovereignty. In a world where the US President signs memorandums to tariff European regulation out of existence, that price is not just economic — it is existential.

Europe has a choice. It can continue the quiet erosion, delaying and simplifying until the Act is a shell. Or it can learn from the GDPR: hold the line, enforce the rules, and let the Brussels Effect do its work. The first path leads to vassalisation. The second leads to sovereignty. There is no third option.
