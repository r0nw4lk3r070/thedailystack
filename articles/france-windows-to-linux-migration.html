France Bets Its Digital Sovereignty on Linux — But Can It Scale What the Gendarmerie Built?

April 2026 · Article · 8 min read

France is leaving Windows. On April 8, the country's digital directorate, DINUM, announced it will migrate government workstations to Linux. The directive is clear: every ministry must produce plans to eliminate "extra-European tech dependencies" by autumn 2026.

Headlines blared "2.5 million devices switching to Linux." The reality is more nuanced. DINUM is starting with its own 350 workstations. The 2.5 million figure represents the total pool of French civil servants covered by the broader sovereignty directive, not a fleet of machines moving this year.

What matters isn't the headline number, but the architecture. France is not just swapping one operating system for another; it is building a full replacement stack—OS, applications, cloud, and identity—designed to exit the Microsoft ecosystem entirely.

The question is whether it can do in two years what took the Gendarmerie twenty.

The Gendarmerie Did It — But It Took Two Decades

If you want proof that a French government institution can run Linux at scale, look at the Gendarmerie nationale. As of June 2024, its custom Ubuntu distribution, GendBuntu, runs on 103,164 workstations—97% of the force's computing estate. The migration saved roughly €2 million a year in licensing costs and reduced the total cost of ownership (TCO) by an estimated 40%.

But the "June 2024" date hides a long history. The Gendarmerie started in 2004. They didn't start with the OS; they began with applications—OpenOffice, Firefox, and Thunderbird—while still running Windows. GendBuntu didn't land on the first workstations until 2008. Full deployment took another sixteen years. The project survived multiple changes of government because it was never a political showcase; it was a slow, methodical, and unglamorous infrastructure programme.

DINUM explicitly cites GendBuntu as the governance model for the national rollout. However, a critical gap remains: how to compress twenty years of institutional learning into a timeline that demands ministry-wide plans in six months and dependency elimination by 2027.

The Stack Behind the Headlines

The OS switch is the visible layer. The real engine is La Suite Numérique—DINUM's sovereign productivity platform.

La Suite already runs Tchap, an encrypted messaging service used by over 600,000 civil servants. In January 2026, DINUM added Visio, a Jitsi-based video conferencing tool replacing Microsoft Teams for 200,000 users. The platform also includes collaborative document editing (Docs), database management (Grist), sovereign webmail, and file storage via FranceTransfert.

The entire stack runs on Outscale servers (a subsidiary of Dassault Systèmes), certified SecNumCloud by ANSSI. This isn't a relabelled American cloud; it is French-hosted, French-audited, and French-certified infrastructure.

This is the core of the strategy. A Linux workstation still running Teams, Outlook, and SharePoint hasn't achieved sovereignty—it has only changed the entry point. By addressing the full dependency chain, France is attempting to exit the ecosystem entirely.

Why Now: The Trump Factor

France's digital sovereignty push predates the current US administration, but the urgency has spiked. The Trump administration's weaponisation of sanctions and trade policy made a hard truth clear to European governments: dependence on American technology is a security vulnerability.

The escalation has been steady. Trump-era tariffs reignited the cloud sovereignty conversation in April 2025, leading to record growth for local providers like OVHcloud and Scaleway. By January 2026, the EU Parliament voted to identify areas of foreign digital dependency. The April 8 Linux announcement is the OS layer of this broader geopolitical shield.

Minister David Amiel framed it plainly: "Regain control of our digital destiny." This is sovereignty language, not cost-saving language. The €2 million saved by the Gendarmerie supports the argument, but it doesn't drive it.

Microsoft has declined to comment. Their silence is notable, given they have aggressively fought previous government migrations—most famously in Munich, where a decade-long LiMux project was eventually reversed under sustained vendor pressure.

The Sovereignty Paradox

Here is the tension: the Linux kernel—the foundation of every distribution France might adopt—is built primarily by American companies.

Linux Foundation data shows that the top contributing organisations remain US-centric: Meta, Intel, Red Hat, and Google. SUSE is the only European company in the kernel top 10. France is effectively swapping a contractual dependence on Microsoft for a structural dependence on a US-dominated development ecosystem.

The counterargument is that open source allows France to fork the kernel, audit the code, and run its own build pipeline. None of these options exist with Windows. But "influence" is not "control." If US giants prioritise features that serve their own business models, France cannot redirect development. The dependency is structural rather than contractual—a different shape of dependence, not an absence of it.

France has chosen the first definition of sovereignty (no lock-in) and is betting that the second (structural dependency) can be mitigated over time through European contributions.

Others Have Tried

France is not alone. Schleswig-Holstein in Germany began a similar transition in 2024, completing nearly 80% of a 30,000-workstation migration by early 2026. While some reports cite savings of €15 million against a €9 million investment, these figures vary across sources and should be viewed as approximations of the potential ROI.

There are also cautionary tales. Munich's LiMux project failed after a decade. Various Italian and Spanish regional governments announced migrations and quietly reversed course. The failure pattern is consistent: "big-bang" approaches, insufficient internal support, and political shifts that removed executive sponsorship.

France is attempting to avoid these traps by referencing the Netherlands' CommonGround initiative and Germany's Sovereign Cloud Stack (SCS), though the expiration of SCS federal funding in 2025 suggests France may be building its sovereign stack largely alone.

Forrester's Warning

Forrester's 2026 European Predictions report delivers a blunt assessment: no European company will be able to completely move away from US hyperscalers by 2026. A complete switch from AWS, Google Cloud, or Azure to local providers is deemed unrealistic in the short to medium term.

Forrester recommends pursuing sovereignty that is "feasible, desirable, sustainable, and affordable"—not absolute independence. The question is whether government institutions, backed by legislation and taxpayer funding, face the same constraints as private companies. They likely don't, but they aren't exempt from the laws of technical gravity.

What Happens Next

The next milestone is June 2026, when DINUM convenes "Industrial Digital Meetings" to formalise public-private coalitions. By autumn, every ministry must submit reduction plans covering eight categories: workstations, collaborative tools, security software, AI, databases, virtualisation, cloud, and telecoms.

The stakes are straightforward. If France pulls this off—the full-stack migration from Windows to Linux, Teams to Visio, and Azure to Outscale—it becomes the most significant rejection of US tech dominance by a Western democracy. It becomes the template for every other European government.

If it fails—if the timeline proves too compressed or political will evaporates—it becomes the next Munich. A cautionary tale of institutional fatigue.

The Gendarmerie proved it can be done. It also proved how long it takes. France is now betting it can accelerate that timeline by an order of magnitude without breaking the governance model that made it work.

That is the real story. Not 2.5 million devices. The bet.
